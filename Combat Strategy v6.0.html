The Standoff

A drawn gun is stronger than a fired one. When to shoot, when to fold, and why the smart boss makes his rival pay to pull the trigger.

Combat isn't how you win — Respect is. But the turf that carries your Contracts, your Deeds, and your Speakeasies rarely comes quietly, and that makes the gun the price of the crown. The trick is knowing that most firefights are decided before a die is rolled — in the standoff, in the maths of who holds the better ground. Here's the ground.

Two Numbers Run Every Fight

Everything comes down to how many dice you roll and what kills:

  • Dice = the Muscle Ratio — the same staircase as brewing: 1–2 mobsters roll 1 die, 3–4 roll 2, 5–6 roll 3, 7–8 roll 4, 9+ roll 5 (the cap). More bodies, more lead — but with hard diminishing returns.
  • Threat = the kill number. Base kills on a 5–6 (one-third of dice). Each +1 Threat widens it: Level 2 kills on 4+, Level 3 on 3+, Level 4 (the max) on 2+ — five-sixths of every die. Threat matters far more than dice, because it multiplies every die you roll.

And Threat is where the defender lives. A dug-in Occupier stacks bonuses an attacker simply cannot match.

The Standoff: The Whole Game in Two Questions

When you Move onto turf a rival Controls, the fight pauses. First the Occupier answers; then the Invader chooses, and keeps choosing until the Pin breaks.

The Occupier answers first

Ambush

A free shot at +1 Threat before anyone else fires. No Heat. Then the fight is on.

Hold Fire

No shot. The Invader is Pinned but unhurt — a signal: "maybe you're just passing through."

Fold

Give up the block, flee your crew to safe turf. Lose the liquor, keep the bodies. Costs nothing.

Then the Invader chooses — and pays

Open Fire — 1 Influence

Both sides roll at once. If you kill, you take Heat. Still standing? Pay again to fire again.

Advance — 1 Influence

Shoot your way out to safe turf, barrels and all. A fighting retreat, not a rout.

Fall Back — free

Cut and run to where you came from, liquor left behind. Forced on you at 0 Influence.

The asymmetry that defines combat: the Occupier shoots first and free, adds +2 Threat from a Safehouse, and never takes Heat for defending his own corner. The Invader pays Influence for every volley and draws Heat the moment he kills. Defence is the strong position — by a wide margin. Attacking is a premium you pay in dice, in Influence, and in police attention.

Three Principles

1

Never attack into even numbers

The defender's free ambush shot and Safehouse +2 mean a fair fight isn't fair — it's a 1-in-50 for the attacker. If you mean to take a block, bring at least double the garrison, ideally into the next dice tier (5–6 bodies for 3 dice, 9 for the 5-dice cap). Anything less just donates your crew and your Heat to the man you're attacking.

2

The Influence tax caps your persistence

Every Open Fire costs 1 Influence — a Play you're not spending on money or Contracts. Grinding a stubborn alley from 3 volleys to 5 lifts your odds barely (25% → 31%) while your losses climb and rivals run free across the city. Commit hard or not at all: plan the force to win in 2–3 volleys, and if it's not breaking, Fall Back before you're bled dry. There is no cheap war of attrition here.

3

Fold to save the crew; fight only for turf worth blood

Folding costs zero casualties — you trade the block and its barrels for a mobile, intact crew that lives to earn tomorrow. Standing to fight a stronger raider bleeds you whether you hold or not (2–3 casualties average even at a coin-flip). So fold the empty corner without a second thought; dig in only when your hold odds are high, or the district carries a Contract, a Deed, or a Speakeasy you can't win without.

The Occupier's Playbook

When a rival crosses your line, run the read in this order:

Occupier decision: what to do when they Move in
If…Do thisWhy
The block is empty scrap you don't need Fold Keep every body. Turf is cheap; a trained crew isn't. Let them have the corner and make them defend it next.
You want the block and intend to fight Ambush The free +1-Threat shot is pure upside — it strictly beats Hold Fire on both hold-rate and your own losses. If you're fighting at all, shoot first.
You're unsure they mean to fight Hold Fire (a bluff) Not a combat move — a political one. It lets a passer-by leave without a war, and hides your strength. Use it to keep the peace, not to win the fight.
Why the drawn gun beats the fired one. Because defence is so strong, the threat of your garrison does more work than the shooting ever will. A rival who can read the table knows a Secured block with three guards is a 90% wall — so he doesn't attack it; he goes around. Your bodies earn their keep standing still. That is the quiet genius of the standoff: the fights you win are mostly the ones nobody starts.

So What's the Optimal Play?

  • Defending: a Safehouse plus 2–3 Runners and your Boss turns a district into a wall (66–90% hold vs. a real raid). Secure the turf that carries your win — Contract sites, Deeds, Speakeasies — and let the scraps go.
  • Attacking: only with overwhelming force (2–3× the garrison) into turf you genuinely need, and only when you can afford the Influence and the Heat. A raid that stalls is worse than a raid never launched.
  • Above both: most of your combat value is positional — garrisons that deter, standoffs that bluff, folds that preserve. Spend bullets rarely and decisively; spend the threat of them constantly.
Kingpin's Note

The hothead charges a Secured block with an even crew and wonders why he's burying four men and holding a Rat's worth of Heat. The made man garrisons the turf his Contracts need, folds the corners that don't earn, and when he finally does move, he moves with three-to-one and ends it in two volleys. Same dice. One of them dies rich.
