The Whole Racket

This is five guides in one, because Moonshine Kingdom is five games in one, played simultaneously, with the same ten markers. A boss who plays them separately is playing all five badly. The point of binding them into one book is the seams: every chapter ends where another begins, and the games are won in the seams.

The Day, in One Breath

Each Day runs dawn to midnight. Shadows: The city cooks a common mash, the boilers fire or burst, each boss takes his pick of the Jobs Market, and whatever Influence isn't promised elsewhere funds the day's Ledger. The Hustle: Plays around the table, one at a time, until every boss has stepped off the street, and the order they step off decides everything about tomorrow. Reckoning: The cops thin the armies, the Deeds and crowns change hands, and the city checks whether anyone just won.

The One Line That Runs the City

Plays today  =  Influence  −  staked  −  Heat,  up to the Ledger's 5 slots.

Commit that to memory, because every chapter in this book is a fight over one of its three terms. Influence is capacity, not actions: the Ledger stops at five slots, so the markers above your fifth are the ones that stake Jobs and refill the five the Heat Track keeps. Staked is every promise you've made to the Market and not yet kept. Heat is every noise you've made that the law hasn't forgiven yet, and the nightly Reckoning does not forgive it; only a Raid does. Raise the first term, time the second, aim the third. That is the entire game, and the rest of this book is the detail.

The Three Clocks

Under the day loop, three slower clocks tick. The cheap Respect drains one way: completed Jobs leave the deck for good, and the easy tier goes first. The Influence ladder climbs one way: from the 6 markers everyone starts with to the 10 that crown a king, at $2,500 a rung. And the Heat cycle turns over and over: quiet, loud, sirens, refund, quiet. Every plan in this book is really a claim about which clock matters most right now.

The Book, Part by Part

It runs in five parts, in the order the day deals them. Part I, The Brew Engine is how the dawn stills really make their money, why seven is armour, and how to pick the Harbormaster's pocket. Part II, The Jobs Market is why the word on the street never changes, what a promise costs by the day, and when to reach for the card. Part III, The Standoff is when to shoot, when to fold, and why the drawn gun beats the fired one. Part IV, Heat and the Law is how the Raid picks its door and how to pick it first: planting evidence, decoy warehouses, and the fine art of the Rat. Part V, The Table is handshakes, frame-ups, bounties and conspiracies, the whole business of cooperation in a game with no shared victories. The Contents overleaf points at every chapter, and at each play inside them.

A Note on the Figures

The combat and brewing numbers are Monte Carlo estimates from the game's own simulators, 100,000+ trials per figure, rules-accurate down to each mob's signature Plays. The Jobs and Heat numbers are deterministic: read straight off the rules and machine-checked against the live card files. What none of them can model is the table: who reads whose barrels, who blinks in the staring contest, and whether the man holding the card you need can be talked out of it. Those decide more games than the arithmetic does. Hence Part V.

Part I · The Brew Engine

Before the sun clears the tenements, the stills are already breathing. Steam in the pipes, mash in the boilers, and out the back a river of clear liquor that turns into a river of dirty cash. It looks like luck: a roll of the bones and a prayer. It isn't. Behind the dice sits a small, sharp piece of arithmetic, and the bosses who read it right out-earn the ones who don't without ever rolling a better number.

The One Rule That Governs Everything

Every dawn the city cooks a common mash. That's the Mash die (grey as the Moonshine it makes), sitting in the open for all to see. Then, in Turn Order, each boss drafts one Red die from the pool and lays it beside the Mash. The two together are your Brew Number, and that number is the tune your empire plays today. Every Still you Control whose boiler shows that number roars to life at once; every Still on a different number sits cold, a dead kettle in a dark room.

Read that twice, because the whole trade falls out of it: the dice only ever hand you one number a day. A boss with a 7-boiler and an 8-boiler doesn't light both off the same roll: one cooks, the other waits its turn. But a boss with two 7-boilers fires both together, off the same lucky pair of dice. Matching numbers stack their yield; mismatched numbers take turns and leave you idle. One lever bends that law, and only one: stand your Boss on a Still that fires, and he can walk the batch to one more Still you Control next door, whatever its number (Split the Batch; this chapter closes on him). So the game of the Brew is deciding which numbers to marry your money to, and, because the old man walks on land, which streets.

And here's the gift: you see tomorrow coming. The Mash for the next dawn is set the night before, at the close of the Shadows, and it sits face-up on the board through the entire day's business. So half of every Brew Number on tomorrow's table, and half of every Blowback that could gut your crew, is public knowledge a full day early. Seizing the Stills that pair with it, walking your Boss out of harm's way, Laying Low early to grab the first draw of the bones: those aren't hunches. They're the sharpest plays on the board, and the intel is already in your hand.

The Hand on the Dice: The Harbormaster

Here is the wrinkle that separates a kingpin from a gambler. That common mash doesn't fall from heaven. It comes ashore in barrels of molasses, up the piers, over the docks; and whoever holds the Harbormaster Title, the crown for Controlling the most Dock Districts, owns that trade. So at the close of each Shadows, the Harbormaster doesn't leave tomorrow's Mash to the roll of a die. He names it. Any face he likes, 1 through 6. Every drop of molasses lands on his wharves, and he decides what the whole town brews at dawn.

That turns half of every rival's Brew Number into a political number: a favour granted or a knife slipped in, depending on whose Stills he means to feed. He will name the face that lights his own boilers and leaves yours stone cold, and because the Mash sits in the open all day, the whole table watches the squeeze coming and can do nothing about it after the fact. The only question left worth asking is the one that wins games: what will he pick, and can he lock me out of my own morning?

The Magic of Seven

The answer to "can they lock me out?" comes down to a single, beautiful quirk of arithmetic. A boiler on number n can only fire if the Mash leaves a draftable Red (1–6) to complete it: that is, if n minus the Mash lands between 1 and 6. Work that through for every boiler number and a clear winner falls out:

Every other clump can be shut clean off: name the right face and a wall of 8-boilers fires nothing at all that morning. Cold iron and idle men. On this board, "just cook sevens" isn't a hunch a made man passes down for luck. It's armour.

So when this chapter says "clump," understand what the board is actually offering: a wall of 8s or 9s isn't merely starvable, it's two boilers. The fat matching stack every boss dreams about exists on exactly one number, and it is the one number the docks can't starve. The catch, and it's a cruel one. There are only four 7-boilers in the whole city, and every last one sits under a High Society address, one to a mainland Borough, none out on Staten Island. Those are the $500 rooms, and they open the game padlocked by the Police until a Raid kicks the doors in (see Part IV). So a lockout-proof engine of sevens means prising cop-guarded, silk-lined turf out of three or four separate Boroughs at once: blood for every boiler. And to deny a rival the Mash on top of that, you'd have to wear the Harbormaster's crown yourself: the most Dock Districts, strung out across the water on the far shore. No single mob can grip a fat wall of sevens and the docks both; the crew stretches until it snaps. You choose a lane. Own the sevens, or own the piers. Almost no one owns both.

The Other Way Out: Spread the Ends

Can't fight your way to the sevens? There's a second road to immunity, and it runs against every instinct that says "clump." Instead of piling onto one number, bracket a low boiler and a high one. A pair like 3 and 9 can never be starved together: the face that kills the 3 (a high Mash) is the very face that feeds the 9, and the other way round. Squeeze one, you open the other.

It costs you something. A spread lights one end a morning, so it never pours like a fat matching stack in a good week; but it always pours something, every single dawn, no matter whose crown sits on the docks, and when a hostile Harbormaster is starving the table that reliability is worth real money. One thing to mind: the low numbers are scarce on the map, just two 2-boilers and two 3-boilers in the whole city, so you bracket the ends. You don't clump them.

And one bracket on the map breaks the see-saw. Not the classic 3-and-9: those four boilers sit in four separate Boroughs, and no two touch. But Whitestone's 5 stares across a bridge at Throggs Neck's 10, both piers. Walk the Boss to whichever end tomorrow's public Mash feeds, and the live end fires while the old man Splits into the dead one: near $740 a Day off ten men, $720 against the cruellest face a crown can name, where the 3-and-9 clears $321. The city's one bracket that touches, and both halves count toward the crown.

Read the Docks: The Crown Can't Starve Itself

For all that dread, the Harbormaster wears a collar of his own making. He cooks Moonshine too (the same common mash the whole city brews), and the face he names lights his boilers exactly as it darkens yours. He cannot reach for a number that starves your Stills without first making sure it won't starve his own. His turf is a leash on the number he can pick, and a boss who reads that leash can walk right up to the crown and pick his pocket. Two things fall out of that, and both are yours to exploit:

  • You can predict tomorrow's Mash by reading the Harbormaster's Stills. A crew cooking high boilers (8s, 9s) will name a mid-to-high face to fire them; they daren't name a low one, because a low Mash leaves their own high Stills cold. Their turf tells you their hand a full Day early.
  • Mirroring is a hard lock. Build your Stills on the same numbers the Harbormaster holds. Whatever face he names to fire his own boilers fires yours too: there is no Mash he can pick that lights his engine and darks a matching one. He can only starve you by starving himself, and a boss who cooks Moonshine won't. Ride the crown's own greed.

This is why the truly spiteful shutout (the 8-stack starved to $0 in the ledgers below) only lands on a crew whose numbers don't overlap the Harbormaster's. Match his range and the worst case softens into "we both brew, every morning." The lesson isn't to fear the docks; it's to watch them.

The Kill Shot: Mirror, Then Lay Low First

Mirroring gets you a seat at the Harbormaster's table. Laying Low first takes his chair. The Harbormaster sets the Mash at the end of Shadows, in the open, before anyone acts. Then, all through the Hustle, tomorrow's Turn Tokens are claimed one by one in the order players Lay Low: step off the board first and you claim Token #1, the first draft of tomorrow's Red pool.

Here's why that's murder against a tight-clumped crown. When the Harbormaster cooks a single number, the face he names to fire it leaves exactly one firing Red in the whole pool: name Mash 2 to light your 8s and only a 6 completes them. That scarcity is the trap he laid for the table. But with first draft, you take that one golden Red. He built the shortage; you pocket the only key.

The price of the chair, and it went up. Laying Low ends your Hustle for the Day: every Play you'd still have made is the fare for Token #1. And The Offers run in reverse Turn Order (see Part II), so Token #1 picks the Jobs Market last: the chair costs today's tempo and tomorrow's pick of the work. Against a crown clumped on one number, 60% against 11% is worth almost any fare; against a spreading one, take the high token: pick the Jobs, and hold the match.

Three Principles of the Brew

1

Clump on a number they can't take from you

Boilers that share a number fire as one, so clumping is how you brew big; but which number you clump on matters as much as how many. A wall of sevens is Harbormaster-proof, and it is also the only wall on the map: seven is the one number with four boilers. A "wall" of 8s or 9s is two kettles and a light switch the docks can flip off at dawn. Clumping past a pair means 7 (four), 4 or 6 (three), and nothing else in the city. Cook seven, or make your peace with a rival who can leave your kettles cold whenever it suits him.

2

Spread your crew, don't pile it on

The Muscle Ratio is a staircase, not a ladder, and the Sweep decides which step you can stand on. Every Reckoning thins any District above 5 Mobsters back to 5, and the Brew comes round the very next dawn, so 5 is all you will ever have on a boiler at first light. Five men brew 3 barrels; so does a sixth, if you could keep him. Five men to a Still is the sweet spot. And only the odd men earn at all: a growing crew brews 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, so the second man and the fourth carry nothing but their own thirst.

That puts a hard roof on the morning: 3 barrels a boiler, and only one crew pushes past it. The Sicilians are Untouchable: the Sweep never thins their ranks, so they alone wall 9 men onto a boiler and climb to the cap of 5. Everyone else buys breadth, and that is no hardship: once a boiler has its five, the next man you hire earns far more opening a second boiler: a matching one fires off the same pair of dice, and one your Boss can walk to fires whatever the dice say.

3

Fight for the docks, or mirror the man who holds them

The Harbormaster's crown is a hand on every rival's throat, but it has two soft spots. It's hard to keep: eight Docks strung across the water and all connected as if one, so no runaway holds the whole waterfront for long. And if you can't take the piers, mirror them, then Lay Low first: build on the crown's own numbers, beat him to the first draw, and lift the one Red his own Mash left in the pool. The docks answer to force or to imitation, never to complaint.

Park the Boss: Split the Batch

One lever the tables leave out: when the Still in your Boss's District fires, you may fire one other Still you Control in a District Connected by Land or Bridge, whatever its number. A second boiler, on a morning nothing could have reached it. But there's a knife to it: a Blowback plays no favourites, and if your number is the one left to burn, the Boss is the first body on the floor, and a crew with no Boss skips The Offers next morning, so rivals pick over the Market while you spend a Play on a Rise.

His number is his payday and his death sentence, and it is the same number. A boiler only lights when the Mash can reach it, and the Blowback comes off that same arithmetic. Stand him on a 7, the one number all six Mash faces complete, and he Splits near half of all mornings and burns on 7.7% of them. Stand him on a 3, which only two faces reach, and he Splits seldom and dies on 2.6%. No square pays often and dies rarely; pick which you are buying.

The Split is worth whatever crew stands on the far boiler, so what you want beside him is a fat Still on a number he doesn't already share. The board draws the example itself: Sugar Hill's 7 fires constantly and sits across the avenue from East Harlem's lone 12: the rarest boiler in the city, dead weight in any other build, and yours whenever the 7 lights. Note what does not work: the four 7s are one to a Borough and no two of them touch. A wall of sevens cannot Split a single barrel. And better still, the Red that fires his Still is the same Red that bursts it if you leave it behind, so lifting it for the Split pulls the assassin off the table in the same motion: the one draft in this game that pays twice.

One hole left, and it costs nothing to know: your seat doesn't matter, until your number doubles. Whatever chair you draft from, the Red you take leaves the pool with you, so if the face you need showed up once, no leftover can ever burn that boiler. You are only in danger from a double: two 4s on a Mash of 3, you lift one, a rival cheerfully leaves the other. That is when the chair matters, and only the last seat helps: he picks from the final two dice, so he alone decides what stays on the table.

The Don is a Play, not a statue. Rise reads like a funeral rite, but it places your Boss in any Safe District whether the chair was empty or not, and it ignores the map: no Connected route, no water to cross, no hostile block to be Pinned in. Two Influence puts the old man wherever tomorrow needs him, which turns Split the Batch into a dial you re-set at will, because tomorrow's Mash is public from tonight: Rise him onto the junction that will actually fire. It is Blowback insurance on the same terms. And because Control shifts the instant a piece lands, a Rise into an empty block is also the cheapest way to flip a far-off Ward for the Recruit discount.

So What's the Optimal Play?

  • You (or nobody) hold the docks. Clump as wide as the census allows, and buy the block next door while you are at it. No hostile face is coming, so every extra boiler earns, and one of them earns twice.
  • A rival holds the docks. Four lockout-proof answers, in order of price: mirror his numbers (cheapest, his own greed feeds you), clump on 7 (brews most, wants High Society turf), a Connected 6/7/8 (the only build on the sheet that gains from his crown, $807), or spread 3/3 + 9/9 with the Boss on the starved end (cheap ground, and he cannot be touched there).
  • Cutthroat, everyone's on you. Fewer boilers, but only because of the guns, never because of the brew: hold what you can defend, on a number they can't dark. And be honest about the trade, because the Sweep caps a boiler at five: you cannot crowd the losses back. You brew less. You just brew it somewhere safe.
Kingpin's Note

The greenhorn scatters his Stills across four Boroughs to "cover more numbers," lights one boiler a day, and feeds the rest to the Blowback. The old hand cooks one number and gets rich. The made man scatters too, but down one street, where the old man can walk a batch from door to door, and a 12 that fires twice a month starts firing whenever the 7 does. And when a rival's Harbormaster turns greedy? He doesn't grumble: he cooks the crown's own numbers, Lays Low ahead of him for the first draw, and lifts the one Red the whole city's been starved of. The Harbormaster set the table. The made man eats first.

Part II · The Jobs Market

Deeds and Titles pay you for what you hold. Jobs pay you for what you do. Five cards lie face-up beside the board, every one a public dare, and none of them ever expires. No deadline, no paperwork: only a stake, sitting where everyone can count it, quietly costing you a Play a day. Most bosses treat the Market like a shop. It isn't. It's a queue, a clock and a confession all at once.

The One Rule That Governs Everything

A Job prints two numbers. Its Respect is what it pays. Its Stake is the Influence you lock out of your Reserves the morning you claim it, and don't get back until you pull it off. But read what a locked marker actually is, because the card doesn't tell you: The Offers run before Fund the Ledger. Stake first, fund second. Every marker sitting on a Job is a marker that never reaches your Ledger, and only Ledger markers buy Plays. So the stake is not a fee. It is rent, and the rent is paid in Plays, every single Day you hold the card. Claim a 5-Respect Job on Monday and pull it off on Thursday, and you have quietly handed your rivals four short days on the street.

The whole system is that one line of arithmetic from the front of this book, Plays today = Influence − staked − Heat, up to five slots, and note what it doesn't say. There is no gate: no Job here is ever unclaimable for want of markers. A big stake costs you actions, not access, one marker for one Play all the way down to a one-Play morning, which makes the fifth slot the only cliff: past it the trade stops costing anything at all.

Heat Is Billed to the Same Account

Now put the third term back, because that table is a fiction and every real morning has Heat in it. A noisy Play doesn't return its marker to your Reserves; it locks it onto the communal Heat Track, where it sits until a Raid clears the board. It is gone from the same pool the stake comes out of, and it is gone across Days: the Reckoning does not clear Heat. Only the sirens do. So the ladder in that table slides down one rung for every marker cooling on the Track. Your working capital is Influence minus Heat, and only the first five of it buys Plays. Two markers hot, and a 6-Influence crew has no free capacity at all: even a 1-Respect card starts costing a Play.

Now count the deck: fourteen of the thirty-two Jobs draw Heat in the act of completing them. All eight Open Fires, the five big Unloads, the lone Extort. The Market sells you cards that burn the very currency you need to claim the next one, and a crew halfway up the Heat Track has no room left to promise anybody anything. That is not an accident. There are only three answers: keep your Jobs quiet, climb the Influence ladder faster than you climb the Heat Track, or let the Raid come, because a Raid returns every Heat marker to its owner's Reserves. The cops kick your door in and refund your stake money on the way out (see Part IV).

Bribe is a Jobs play. Everyone reads Bribe as the last chore before the crown: four payments of $2,500 you make when you're already winning. It's the opposite. The 7th marker makes a 3-Respect Job free to hold; the 8th makes a 5 free; and every marker past that is a rung of headroom against the Heat you'll be generating to complete them. Those markers are the cheapest tempo in the game, they were on the road to the throne anyway, and the boss who buys them on Day 4 plays a different game from the boss who buys them on Day 12. The ladder isn't there to make Jobs free. It's there to keep them free once you're loud.

One Claim a Day Is a Rate, Not a Limit

Nothing caps how many Jobs you hold. The Offers let you claim one card a morning, and there is no deadline on any of them, so a patient boss simply accumulates: a 1 on Tuesday, a 3 on Thursday, a 5 the following week, all three sitting staked in front of him at once. The only ceiling is what your Reserves can carry, and the arithmetic runs on your total staked, not on the newest card. That changes the shape of a plan: a three-card overlap isn't a thing you assemble in an afternoon; it's three separate mornings, and the first card starts paying rent from the first one. But it also means the cheap tier compounds beautifully: two or three 1-Respect Jobs, all riding Plays your liquor business was making anyway, cost a total Stake of 3 and complete whenever the barrels happen to line up. Hold a small standing portfolio and let the board complete it for you.

The Five-Play Ceiling Is a Fiction: The Kickback

One rule breaks the whole funding equation open, and it's easy to file under "money" and forget. Every barrel of Havana Rum you Unload moves one spent Influence marker from your Reserves back into an empty Ledger slot. Not next morning: immediately, mid-Hustle. Moonshine pays cash only; Rum pays cash and gives you your day back. An Unload costs 2 Influence, so the moment you sell more than two Rum you are running a profit in Plays, though the Ledger's five slots cap that profit at +3:

Two limits keep it honest: you can only pull back markers you have already spent this Day, and you can't overfill five slots, so the Kickback tops out at +3 Plays however deep the Rum runs. Both also mean the Kickback is a late-day engine: it does nothing at dawn and everything at dusk, exactly when your Ledger is running dry and the table is deciding who steps off first. Which is where it turns from an economic rule into a political one.

Sell late, and sell in fives. Two corollaries fall straight out of those limits, and both cost bosses money nightly. First, your empty slots are your Kickback capacity: a marker can only come home to a slot that's open, so Unload after you've spent your other markers, when the Ledger sits emptiest, never at dawn with all five slots full and nothing to recycle into. Second, six-plus Rum in a single Unload throws the recycling away: past the fifth barrel there is no slot left to fill, so the surplus sells for cash and nothing more. Pour a fat load all at once and you've handed back the very trait you paid a Dock to earn; hold the extra Rum for another emptied Ledger, and it keeps buying your day back one sale at a time.

And under it all, the Odd Jobs floor. The obvious worry: what good is a marker returned at dusk, when the streets have emptied and every rival has already Laid Low? Cash, is what. Stepping off pays $100 for every marker still in your Ledger, so a Kickback you never find a Play for is simply collected on your way off the street. That puts a hard floor under every barrel of Havana Rum: $400 at a Standard joint, $600 under the chandeliers, sale plus salvage, even on a dead evening. Moonshine has no floor but its sticker. Price the two honestly, and Rum's premium survives the nights when the tempo it buys is worth nothing.

The Market Never Moves, and That's the Whole Design

Nothing on the street ages. No card slides down a row, nothing goes stale, nothing is swept away at dawn. The Market changes only when a player changes it: somebody takes a Job or walks away from one, and a fresh card fills the gap. That single fact rewrites how you're supposed to play.

First: there is no clock but your own money. Since the card cannot expire, claiming it early buys you exactly one thing (certainty that a rival won't take it), and you pay for that certainty in Plays, daily, until you fire. Which produces the doctrine this whole chapter is built on:

The Claim-Late Doctrine

Every Job in the deck completes in a single Play. So build the position first, in silence, with your Reserves full and your Ledger fat, and claim the card on the morning you can fire it that same afternoon. One Day of rent, full Respect. A 5 taken and finished on the same Day costs you two Plays. The same 5 claimed three Days early costs you six.

Second: the Market lets you plan in silence, and the tell is your board, not your hand. Because the card will still be there next week, the correct play against a card you want is often to say nothing and move barrels. But understand what you're hiding and what you're not. The Job is public. The stake is public. Your intention is the only secret you own, and it leaks through the board, not the Market. Six barrels drifting toward Fordham, a Safehouse creeping into a rival's Ward, your Boss walking into Queens: a boss who reads turf can see a claim coming a full Day before you make it, and take the card out from under you.

Third: a named Job is a standing bounty. Under a churning market, the correct answer to Kill a rival Boss in Queens sitting face-up would be to wait for it to rot off the end of the row. It doesn't rot. The Toll Booth Trap sits there, unclaimed, until somebody is man enough to take it; and until then, every Boss on the board simply avoids Queens. That is the deck doing work with cards nobody has even claimed, and it is why the four headline bounties are named by borough. Read the Market as a map of where not to stand.

Fourth: a claim is a lie you can afford to tell. Everything above treats the public Job as a leak to be plugged, but a leak runs whichever way you aim it. Claiming a Job is the one move that lets you publish an intention, so publish a false one. Take a card that sends you into Manhattan (a Boss to kill, a block to seize) and the boss with turf there reads the war coming and thickens his garrison, pulling guns toward the noise. Your real road runs the other way: out to the neutral piers of Staten Island, nobody's home Borough and nobody's first worry, where a quiet Dock or two hands you the Harbormaster and with it the power to name the city's Mash. The feint costs exactly what the Claim-Late Doctrine warned you about: rent on a card you may never fire, only here the rent is the point. You aren't buying the Respect; you're buying three rivals looking the wrong way for a Day. The loudest thing on the table can be the card you never meant to keep.

The Staring Contest, and What It's Actually About

Late in the Hustle, with two markers left and nothing urgent to spend them on, you are in a staring contest. Step off now and you take the low token: tomorrow's dice and tomorrow's tempo, and a Market picked over by the time it reaches you. Burn a Play you don't strictly need and you outlast the man beside you for first refusal on the street. But tempo in the abstract is almost never what the contest is about; it sharpens on exactly one kind of night: when a card is face-up that two of you want, and both of you know it. Then the queue is the whole game, and it is won three ways: by reading him, by hiding yourself, and by having the Rum to outlast him.

Layer one: read what they're building toward

A rival's plan is written in two places, and neither of them is his face. His staked hand is public, so you already know what he needs; his board tells you when he'll be ready: barrels drifting toward one Speakeasy, a Safehouse creeping off his home turf, bodies gathering two blocks from a Dock. Put the two together and you can date his claim to within a Day. Then do the same for the cards he hasn't claimed, the harder read and the more valuable one, because it lets you deny him something he hasn't paid for yet. A crew massing Rum in Red Hook for four Days is telling you which card it wants off the street, whether or not that card is in its hand.

Layer two: don't be readable yourself

Everything above is being done to you. The Claim-Late Doctrine says build first and claim on the morning you fire, but that only works if the build is quiet, because the window in which you're vulnerable is precisely the window in which you're visibly ready and don't own the card yet.

  • Assemble late and assemble fast. Six barrels arriving in Fordham across four Days is an announcement. Six barrels arriving in one Move, on the morning you claim, is a fait accompli.
  • Don't let the last Play be the obvious one. A position that only makes sense as a run at one card has already told them. Stage from somewhere that threatens two things.
  • Weigh claiming early anyway. You cannot hide a Safehouse walking across the map. When the build is inherently loud, pay the rent and lock the card; the alternative is doing the work for someone else.
  • Or be the Vipers. Whispers claims the deck's top card face-down. Rivals see a Stake and nothing else: the only way in the game to hold a Job that cannot be read, denied, or pre-empted.

Layer three: Rum wins the staring contest

And here is the lever that turns the whole thing from a guessing game into a plan. Everyone else's day ends when their Ledger runs dry. A crew holding Rum decides when its day ends: three barrels sold and you are a Play up, silently. So when a card you both want is sitting on the street, you don't have to hope he blinks; you Unload, refill, outlast him, and take first pick at dawn. At a Standard joint Rum pays the same cash as Moonshine, which is why a crew that never Trades sees nothing on its ledger sheet and cannot understand why it keeps reaching the Market last. Trade up, or spend the game explaining why the good cards keep going to somebody else.

When to blink first anyway

  • Step off early when tomorrow's Mash is a number you own, when the pool looks likely to leave a Blowback on your own boiler, or when there is simply nothing on the street you could complete this week. Dice beat cards when the cards don't fit you.
  • Hang on to the end when a card you can fire tomorrow is face-up, when a rival is visibly one Play from a Job you both want, or when you're the Harbormaster and named tomorrow's Mash yourself: you already know what the dice will do, so spend the token on the Market instead.
  • Remember Odd Jobs. Stepping off with markers still in the Ledger pays $100 apiece, the consolation prize for losing the contest, and on a Day you're saving toward a Bribe it's reason enough to blink first: the Bribe buys the queue back permanently.

No Boss, no business. A crew with no Boss on the board skips The Offers entirely: no claim, no walk away, nothing, until they spend a Play to Rise. So killing a rival's Boss is not merely a Respect card and a −1 to his Threat: it deletes him from tomorrow's queue. If you and he are racing for the same card, the cheapest way to win the race is to shoot the man out of it. The Blowback will do it for free if he parks the old man on a doubled number, so watch the pool as closely as you watch the Market. But price the threat honestly, because Rise is not only a funeral rite: it places your Boss in any Safe District whether or not the chair was empty, so a Don who sees the knife coming can be two Influence and one Play out of reach before it lands. The queue you delete is the queue of a boss who didn't see it.

Muscle: you don't have to win, you only have to kill

The rule governing every combat Job is more generous than it looks: a Job is a deed, not a board position, and the deed counts even if it didn't survive the Play. Kill the man, lose the block, and the Job still pays; a suicide raid that murders two dockhands and gets thrown back into the river is a completed Job. Only cards that say Seize, take over or destroy need the outcome to stick. What makes them hard is the arithmetic on the other side: a firefight is one Play however many rounds it runs, but every round costs its own Influence, so the real ceiling on a combat Job is the Ledger's five slots. Stake 3 for a headline kill-count Job at 6 Influence and you walk in with three markers, one to enter and two to shoot, against a garrison Part III says needs three-to-one and three volleys to break.

Estate: the cards that move your whole game

Six cards ask you to put your Safehouse somewhere specific, and since Recruit only happens at your Safehouse, that is never a cosmetic request: you are agreeing to rebuild your spawn point in somebody else's neighbourhood, which is a beachhead, not an errand (Part III prices it). Their compensation is that they are the only Respect in the deck nobody can interrupt, because no rival stands between you and your own Safehouse. When the board has turned hostile and the muscle cards are out of reach, Estate is where a losing position can still bank points.

The odd three: Rise, Rat, Extort

One card each, and each carries a trap the other twenty-nine don't. The Empty Casket pays for anointing a successor after your Boss is killed, and you cannot claim it after the fact: no Boss means no Offers at all, so by the time you've Risen the deed is behind you and the card is unclaimable forever. It is insurance, not consolation. Stake 1, take it on a quiet morning, keep a Ward in the family, and forget it. The Insurance Job wants you to Rat and steer the bust onto a low-Pressure Still you Control, which is Lever Three of Part IV run in reverse; and it is the one card in the deck that pays for its own consequences, since its 3 Respect is exactly the −3 the Rat Card will charge you at the Reckoning. Take the call, take the card, come out level, and keep the Raid you bought. And The Five Families wants an Extort touching all five Boroughs in one Play, which means Staten Island: five Respect for a footprint nobody else bothers to build, and it always draws Heat.

Denial: Taking a Card You Never Intend to Do

You may claim any Job on the street. Nothing says you have to complete it. Take the card a rival has spent four Days building toward and it is simply gone: not discarded, not recycled, just locked in your hand for as long as you care to pay for it. This is real and it wins games. It is also priced, and you should know the bill before you reach. It costs your entire Offer, twice: Take and Walk Away are one of three, never two, so denying a card burns the morning you take it and a second morning to shed it. It costs the stake, every Day in between: at 6 Influence, sitting on a rival's 5 costs you two Plays a Day for as long as the grudge lasts. It costs the queue: a shorter Ledger walks you off the street early, so tomorrow the rival you just robbed picks before you. And it doesn't stop the deed: he can still do the thing, he just doesn't get paid for it. So below ten Influence, hate-drafting is a genuine sacrifice, reserved for the moment it decides the game: a rival on 12 or 13 Respect reaching for the card that crowns him. Then it is cheap at any price, because there is no tomorrow to save Plays for.

The one that isn't a sacrifice. A kingpin at 10 Influence has five markers with nothing else to do. He can take the deck's best card, hold it forever, never complete it, and lose nothing at all: his Ledger still fills, his day is still five Plays long, and his markers still count toward the crown wherever they sit. If the leader is at ten and the Market is thin, expect the good cards to start disappearing into a hand that will never play them. The counter is not to out-bid him; it is to get to ten yourself, or to end the game before he's comfortable there.

Three Principles of the Offers

1

Set up in silence; claim on the morning you fire

Every Job in the deck completes in a single Play, and nothing on the street ever expires. Those two facts together mean the stake is a rent you should almost always refuse to pay. Build the barrels, walk the Safehouse, mass the bodies; then take the card at The Offers and finish it that afternoon. One Day of rent instead of five. The only reasons to claim early are denial, or a rival visibly reaching for the same card; and if it's the latter, remember that the tell was never the Market. It was your board, and he was reading it three Days ago.

2

Climb the Influence ladder before you shop

Plays = Influence − staked − Heat, up to five slots. Nothing is ever unaffordable (a stake costs you actions, not access), but below the fifth slot every marker you promise or scorch is a Play you don't make. The markers above the fifth are the free ones, and they're bought with Bribes you were making anyway: staked markers still count toward your ten, wherever they sit on the night. There is no cheaper tempo in the game. And keep the account honest, because the third term is the one that ruins plans: Heat comes out of the same pocket, and the Reckoning doesn't clear it. Run the loud Jobs in bursts, keep quiet ones staked in between, and remember the Raid is a refund.

3

Know which half of the morning you're buying

The Brew drafts forwards and the Market picks backwards, so your Turn Token is a trade, not a rank. Lay Low first and you buy tomorrow's dice and tomorrow's tempo, and surrender the pick of the street. Lay Low last and you buy the Market, and take the leftover Red with the match in your hand. Neither is better; what's fatal is not choosing. And watch the loop that decides it for you if you don't: a heavy stake shortens your Ledger, a short Ledger walks you off the street early, and an early exit puts you at the back of tomorrow's queue. The Market taxes the man who's already been shopping. Two things buy you out of it: ten Influence, and Rum.

Holdings top out at 14 Respect (four Deeds and three Titles is not fifteen), so every rival must come through the Market to win. That makes the Market the last chokepoint in the game, and at 10 Influence you can hold it shut for nothing.
You're behind, and the 1s are gone Take the early Lay Low and rebuild the engine; hunt kill-count cards, not Seize cards. Stepping off first buys the best Red in the city and the first Play of the Hustle, which is how a broken economy restarts. And when you do fight, remember the deed pays even when the raid fails: you don't have to win, you only have to kill. A losing crew can still bank 5 Respect on a suicide run.

The through-line: the Market is not a shop, it's a queue you pay to stand in. The skill isn't spotting the fattest card; everyone can see all of them, and that's the point. It's knowing exactly which morning to reach, and having climbed the Influence ladder far enough that reaching costs you nothing.

Kingpin's Note

The greenhorn grabs the biggest card on the street the day he sees it, pays two Plays a morning for a week, tells the whole table what he's building, and watches the target walk out of the borough. The old hand takes the small ones, the ones his liquor business was doing anyway, and quietly banks four Respect while the shooting goes on somewhere else. The made man does one better: he buys the Commission early, because at ten Influence a promise costs him nothing he'd have spent (so long as he keeps the Track cool); then he sets up in silence, claims on the morning he fires, and takes the last card off the street on the night the second-place man needed it. Nothing in the Market ever expires. Neither does a grudge.

Part III · The Standoff

Guns don't win this war; Respect does. But the turf that carries your Jobs, Deeds and Speakeasies never comes quietly, which makes a loaded pistol the down-payment on the crown. The secret the corner boys never learn: most firefights are settled before a die leaves the hand, in the cold sum of who holds the better ground.

Two Numbers Run Every Fight

Everything comes down to how many dice you roll and what kills. Dice = the Muscle Ratio, the same staircase as brewing: 1–2 mobsters roll 1 die, 3–4 roll 2, 5–6 roll 3, 7–8 roll 4, 9+ roll 5, the cap. Threat = the kill number, base 5–6, each +1 widening it to Level 4's 2+, five-sixths of every die. Threat matters far more than dice, because it multiplies every die you roll, and Threat is where the defender lives.

The Standoff: The Whole Game in Two Questions

When you Move onto turf a rival Controls, the fight pauses: the Occupier answers, then the Invader chooses, and keeps choosing until the Pin breaks.

Ambush – 1 Influence

A first shot at +1 Threat before anyone else fires — Level 4 behind a Boss and Safehouse. Firing first, you take the fight's one Heat marker; spent out or laid low, you can't fire it.

Hold Fire

No shot. The Invader is Pinned but unhurt, a signal: "maybe you're just passing through."

Fold

Give up the block, flee your crew to safe turf. Lose the liquor, keep the bodies. Costs nothing.

Open Fire – 1 Influence

Both sides roll at once. If the Occupier Held Fire, your opening volley draws the fight's one Heat marker; if he Ambushed, it's already his. Still standing? Pay again to fire again.

Advance – 1 Influence

Shoot your way out to safe turf, barrels and all. A fighting retreat, not a rout.

Fall Back – free

Cut and run to where you came from, liquor left behind. Forced on you at 0 Influence.

The asymmetry that defines combat: the Occupier shoots first and adds +1 Threat from a Safehouse, but the Ambusher takes the fight's one Heat marker, so an Invader who storms a defended block draws none. Defence stays the strong position by a wide margin, provided the defender has a marker spare to fire the Ambush.

Three Principles of the Street

1

Never attack into even numbers

The defender's ambush shot and Safehouse +1 mean a fair fight isn't fair: it's a 1-in-70 for the attacker, as long as he can still fire it. If you mean to take a block on even numbers, either bring at least double the garrison (ideally into the next dice tier: 5–6 bodies for 3 dice, 9 for the 5-dice cap), or strike after he's spent his Ledger or laid low and the Ambush never comes (see the Playbook). Anything less, against a defender with a marker in hand, just donates your crew to the man you're attacking.

2

The Influence tax caps your persistence

Every Open Fire costs 1 Influence: a Play you're not spending on money or Jobs. Grinding a stubborn alley from 3 volleys to 4 buys eight points (23% → 32%) while your losses climb and rivals run free across the city. Commit hard or not at all: Plan the force to win in 2–3 volleys, and if it's not breaking, Fall Back before you're bled dry. There is no cheap war of attrition here.

3

Fold to save the crew; fight only for turf worth blood

Folding costs zero casualties and zero Heat: you trade the block and its barrels for a mobile, intact crew that lives to earn tomorrow. Standing to fight a stronger raider bleeds you whether you hold or not (around 3 casualties average even at a coin-flip), and now the Ambush that bleeds him back puts your marker on the Track and the law on you. So fold the empty corner without a second thought, and fold the losing corner too, rather than pay to scratch a raider you can't stop. Dig in and Ambush only when your hold odds are real, or the block is worth a crown: a district your staked Jobs name, a Borough Deed, a Ward or Dock that swings a Title, or a Speakeasy your Night Mayor race can't spare.

Why the drawn gun beats the fired one. Because defence is so strong, the threat of your garrison does more work than the shooting ever will: a rival who can read the table sees a Secured block with three guards as a 90% wall and goes around. Your bodies earn their keep standing still: the fights you win are mostly the ones nobody starts.

The Duck Window: Timing the Lay Low

Duck status is a Ledger state, so it is transient: the morning refill ends it, and the window only runs from your last spent marker (or your Lay Low) to the end of the Day. What decides the risk is how much street is left when it opens, so count the Ledgers still in play before you step off. A raid needs a Move to cross the line and a marker for every volley after, and losing the Ambush never meant losing the fight: the garrison still returns fire, Safehouse and Boss bonuses intact. A rival down to one marker cannot touch a defended block at all (he'd cross, stall at the Standoff, and Fall Back broke); two buy him a crossing and a single volley into return fire. So stepping off a Play or two early, behind garrisons, risks almost nothing. Stepping off at noon, behind blocks you mean to keep, while rivals sit on full Ledgers: that is how strong districts die, and it posts the ~30-point discount above for anyone with an afternoon left to spend. And know the line between the two duck states: spent out is not Laid Low. Running dry, even on an Ambush fired with your last marker, never sends you home mid-fight: an empty Ledger keeps returning fire for free and still takes its turn. The Standoff answer and every return volley are fought inside the raider's Play, never on a turn of your own, so even a crew that has gone home defends itself. If both crews leave a firefight broke, tomorrow's tokens fall in today's turn order.

One boss breaks that arithmetic: the one with Havana stacked at his Speakeasy. The Kickback (Part II) re-arms a dusk Ledger by up to +3 Plays, exactly after the ducks have gone home and nobody is left to answer for them. Five Rum, then a Move and volleys into blocks that can no longer fire first, is the sharpest use of the window in the game. He wears real Heat for it, but he is buying districts with markers nobody budgeted for. While that crew is still out, count his barrels, not his markers, before you claim your token.

The Beachhead: Armies That Don't March

Secure places your Safehouse in any Safe District for $500: no route, no convoy, Control the moment it lands. And since Recruit only happens at your Safehouse, the Play after that hires fresh muscle straight onto the new block, at $500 a man less $100 for every Ward you hold. That is the other invasion route: not five men walking three streets through every Standoff, but a house that appears next door and starts hiring, and the Estate Jobs in Part II pay Respect for it. Three cautions are the whole price: the District must be Safe, so a beachhead lands on empty ground, never on a rival; the old spawn point is abandoned the moment the new one opens; and an unguarded Safehouse is a free takeover, so the first recruits stand guard. Rise is the officer's version: two Influence lands the Boss anywhere Safe, Control on touchdown, and the Split's junction moves with him. Read it in reverse, too: a rival's Safehouse landing two blocks from your Deed isn't a house, it's artillery. The Standoff taxes armies at the door. The beachhead never uses the door.

Everyone else's garrison is capped by the Sweep at 5 mobsters. Torch is a siege tool: 2 Influence, a sacrificed Runner and Heat to burn the Safehouse, lifting tomorrow's assault on a Secured block from 11% to 29% until the rival re-Secures. Prep, not takeover, and it bites hardest alongside a dead Boss, because Rise no longer rebuilds the house: a decapitated crew needs two Plays and $500 before it can Recruit a single man.

So What's the Optimal Play?

  • Defending: a Safehouse plus 2–3 Runners and your Boss is a wall (74–92% hold). Secure the turf that carries your win and let the scraps go.
  • Attacking: only with 2–3× the garrison, into turf you genuinely need, and only when you can afford the Influence and the Heat.
  • Above both: most of your combat value is positional. Spend bullets rarely; spend the threat of them constantly.

Part IV · Heat and the Law

Every chapter in this book keeps arriving at the same line of arithmetic, and the third term is the one nobody budgets for. The Heat Track is the only bank in the city where all four mobs keep their money in one drawer: five spaces, filling a loud Play at a time, until the sirens empty it back into everyone's pockets at once. Most bosses treat it as weather. It isn't. It's a machine with a trigger, a targeting system, and a refund policy, and a boss who reads them can aim the law the way he aims a gun.

What Heat Actually Costs You

A marker on the Track came out of the same ten that fund your Ledger, stake your Jobs, and buy your crown, and it is not coming back at nightfall. The Reckoning does not clear Heat. Only a Raid does. So every hot marker is a rung off the ladder in Part II, this morning and every morning until the sirens. Two markers cooling and a 6-Influence crew can't carry a 1-Respect Job for free anymore; three, and their whole day is shorter than a kingpin's afternoon.

But note the one thing Heat does not cost you, because it changes the endgame: markers on the Track still count toward your ten. The win check counts your Influence wherever it sits: Reserves, Ledger, staked on Jobs, or cooling in a cell. The law taxes your days. It cannot touch your throne. A boss one Bribe from the crown does not need a cool Track; he needs a Reckoning where the count comes up ten, and the Track counts.

The Sicilian dial. Consigliere is the only Play in the game that turns the Track down: one Influence, one phone call, and any marker of the Syndicate's choosing goes home to its owner's Reserves. Read that again: any marker, anyone's. Cooling their own name is the obvious use. The other use is the one to fear, and it's in the Raid rules below: the law hunts the freshest marker, so removing the man in front of you from the queue re-aims the coming Raid at whoever stood behind him. The Syndicate doesn't just quiet the city. It decides who the city is loud about.

How the Raid Picks Its Door

The fifth marker lands, or somebody Rats, and the city locks down. What happens next is completely deterministic (no dice, no discretion), and that is the single most important fact about it, because anything deterministic can be engineered. Each of the four Squads, in the numbered Borough order on the board, does this: it looks at the Districts directly connected to it, inside its own Borough. That is its whole world. Among the mobs holding those districts, it hunts the one whose marker sits furthest right on the Heat Track; if none of them has a marker on the Track at all, it stays put. Then it picks the door: that mob's in-reach district with the most barrels (tie: the hottest boiler on the Borough's Pressure Strip), takes one step in, destroys any Safehouse, seizes every barrel, and scatters the crew. Then it squats there, an impassable wall, until the next Raid moves it on. When all four Squads have moved, every Heat marker on the Track goes home.

Three levers fall out of that paragraph, and they are the difference between a boss the law happens to and a boss who uses it.

Lever one: the law hunts the freshest name, not the worst one

The Squad doesn't count your markers. It reads the Track right to left and takes the first name it recognises: the most recent noise wins, however much older noise sits behind it. Three markers deep but quiet since Tuesday? The law walks past you to the crew that shouted this morning. One marker, placed last? You're the headline. So the discipline isn't "stay quiet"; nobody holding loud Jobs can afford that. It's be loud early in the cycle, then let your rivals shout over you. Every marker anyone else adds after yours is armour, and the boss who does his shooting the morning after a Raid, with the Track empty and four clean names behind him, has bought the longest quiet spell in the game: the whole table must out-shout him before the law comes knocking.

Lever two: reach is geography, and geography is yours to choose

A Squad's reach is one step, inside its own Borough. It cannot cross a Borough line, it cannot jump the water, and it cannot hunt what it cannot touch. Hold no district connected to a Squad and you are untouchable at any temperature: the hottest crew in the city, walking free, because the law can't reach the block you actually stand on. Read the board with that eye and the safe rooms light up: every district more than one step from a Squad, every district across a Borough line from one, and the whole of Staten Island, where no Squad is ever posted. The Staten lane from Part II isn't just uncontested by rivals; it is the one place in the game the law cannot follow you, which is worth remembering when your plan says "be the loudest crew in town." One caution: Squads resolve in Borough order, and a crew scattering from the first can land in the path of the second. When the Track sits at four, check your exits against every precinct, not just the nearest.

Lever three: plant the evidence, the decoy warehouse

You often can't stop the knock. You can almost always choose the door, because the Squad kicks in your most-barrelled district in its reach, and where your barrels sit is entirely up to you. So when your name is going to be freshest (and a boss running loud Jobs always knows when that is), you build the bust before the law does: pick the in-reach district you can best afford to lose, and make it the fattest target on the map. Pile the barrels you were about to sell anyway. Keep the Safehouse off it: the house is $500, a Play, and your only Recruit anchor, and the Raid burns it for free. Keep the Boss off it. Then let them come: the cops seize the plant, padlock a corner you'd already written off, and march straight past the empire. The fee is the barrels (so plant cheap Moonshine, not Rum) and the prize is everything else. One refinement for the connoisseur: the Squad has to step off something to step onto your plant. Early in the game that's a High Society door swinging open, and Part II already told you who's waiting outside it. One thing to mind on the way: the Squad padlocks whatever Still sits under the block it lands on, shutting that boiler cold until a later Raid throws it open, so build the plant on a corner whose brew you can spare.

Leave a back door, or don't leave at all. A raided crew flees on foot to one Land Connected Safe District; and if there isn't one, because every exit is rival turf, water, the Blue Wall or the map's edge, the whole crew is arrested and boxed. Note the trap in that: a Dock's sea lanes are no help here, so a pier with one land exit is a cell with a view. That rule cuts twice. Defensively: never let a barrel pile and a dead-end share an address in Raid season. Offensively: the exits of a rival's likely bust are a thing you can quietly close. A Runner on this corner, a picket on that one, and the Raid you both saw coming stops being a fine and becomes a massacre. The law does the shooting. You just locked the doors.

When You Want the Fire

Everything above treats the Raid as a threat. Half the time it's the payoff. Every Heat marker at the table goes home, and the deepest refund flows to whoever banked the most noise: three markers cooling is three rungs of your ladder back in one breath. The doors open, because each Squad that moves un-padlocks what it was sitting on, and the game begins with all four $500 rooms and all four 7-boilers under police boots: there is no High Society lane and no wall of sevens until somebody makes the city boil. And the board rearranges: safehouses burn, barrels vanish, garrisons scatter into each other's path. The most violent Play in the game, and nobody pays Influence for it.

So the real question about the fifth marker is never whether it lands. It's who chooses the moment, because the boss who places it fires every consequence above with the board arranged his way: his decoy fat, his exits open, his rivals' barrels massed for tomorrow's sales. The catch is printed in Lever One: the trigger marker is by definition the freshest name on the Track, so the trigger man is the target, unless his turf is out of reach, his decoy is already built, or the marker isn't his at all (see Part V). At four markers the whole Hustle changes character: every loud Play is a finger on the trigger, and the boss with a clean position and a reason to want sirens should be racing to pull it first.

The Rat: A Raid on Demand

And then there's the clean trigger. The Rat Play fires an immediate Raid at any Track height, and here are its teeth: it places no marker. The Track stays exactly as the last loud Play left it, so the law hunts whoever holds the freshest marker, not you. A rival storms a block that Holds Fire, Opens Fire and kills three men, masses his barrels for the morning sale, and you drop a dime that same afternoon: he shot first, so the marker is his, his crew is in reach, his stockpile is the fattest door on the block. Not a defensive rule; a precision air strike, purchasable for 2 Influence.

Because the price is the Rat Card: −3 Respect and no second call, until it leaves you; and it leaves exactly one way, when a rival Rats it off you, free and on somebody else's schedule. There is no absolution to buy. That single fact is the whole gamble, and it makes the Card cheaper than it looks in the only way that matters: the books open at Reckoning. Nothing is deducted before then. So the brand is literally free until the night somebody could be crowned, and it prices itself, without a clock, a track or an escalation clause. Early, while the Respect pile is thin, it costs a number nobody is counting yet, and The Insurance Job pays 3 Respect for the call if you engineer the bust onto a low-Pressure Still you Control (Lever Three, run in reverse), netting the whole thing to zero. Late, −3 is a Deed and a half off a 15 you were reaching for. But "early or late" is the crude read. Four sharper ones:

  • It is a bill, not a blackout. The Market still deals to you, the Jobs staked in front of you still complete, the crown is still yours to take. You owe one number, once, on the night the books open. So the question was never can I afford to Rat; it is how many Reckonings away is my 15. Far away, the Card is free. One night away, it is most of a Borough.
  • A rat can still hate-draft. Part II calls the Market the last chokepoint in the game, because holdings top out at 14 Respect and every rival must come through it. The brand no longer spends that veto: you can drop the dime and take the card that would have crowned the runner-up, on the same board, in the same week. That is what makes the Rat a late weapon at all: it hurts the man in front without costing you the tool you finish him with.
  • −3 is a leader's ruin and a fourth-place rounding error. The number is flat, so it bites in inverse proportion to how well you're doing: everything to a boss at 14, nothing at all to a boss at 8 who was never counting to 15 this game. That asymmetry is the design. The Card drifts down the table as the night closes, because the only man who will take it off you is one who has stopped caring what it costs.
  • Where the Card sits decides whether the table has a fire alarm. The only exit needs somebody else to want a Raid, and whoever takes it inherits the −3. Parked on a contender, it is a hostage he badly wants ransomed and no rival has any reason to ransom. Parked on the crew in last, it costs so little that he is in no hurry to move it on either. Either way the on-demand Raid is out of service while it sits there, and the man holding it knows exactly how long everyone else can wait.
Kingpin's Note

The greenhorn thinks Heat is a punishment and plays scared of his own gun. The old hand budgets it: he batches his noise, shouts early in the cycle, and keeps a borough line between his boilers and the blue wall. The made man aims it. He stacks his plant on a corner he never loved, walks the cops off the velvet room, collects his refund with the doors standing open; and when a rival makes the mistake of being loud and rich on the same afternoon, he finds a telephone. The law is a hammer lying in the middle of the table, and every fifth loud Play, somebody gets to pick it up. Make sure it's you.

Part V · The Table

Everything else in this book you can calculate. This chapter you have to play. The rules hand you two instruments (a handshake and an off-book cash trade) and one warning printed in iron: there are no shared victories. Every deal in this game runs out at dusk, every partner is a rival on a schedule, and yet the tables in the other four chapters keep saying the same thing: the man who plays alone pays full price for everything. Here is how to buy cheaper.

Why Deal at All?

Because tempo is zero-sum, and a deal moves it. When two crews trade something each values less for something each values more (your spare Mash face for his walk-away at the Offers, your $1,000 for safe passage through Brooklyn), the tempo they save comes out of the two crews not at the table. Every fair deal is a conspiracy against the men not in it. That is the whole theory of cooperation in this game, and it's why the strongest tables are never quiet: a boss who won't deal isn't being careful, he's paying retail in a city that runs on wholesale.

And because the leader problem cannot be solved alone. Respect is fully public (completed Jobs, Deeds and Titles all sit face-up) and holdings top out at 14, so every crew must come through the Jobs Market to win. When one boss reaches 12, the counterplay is written in Part II: deny the crowning card. But below ten Influence a hate-draft costs the drafter two mornings and days of rent, which no single rival can afford to pay twice, so the containment has to be shared. A cartel of denial, each rival burning one Offer in rotation, splits a crippling bill into three affordable ones. The same logic runs everywhere: the table can leave the leader's number in the pool for the Blowback, the Harbormaster can name the face that starves him, and the man with the freshest marker on the Heat Track can be handed to the law by a single phone call. None of it needs a treaty. It needs three bosses reading the same board and each doing the cheap piece. Do it early: at ten Influence the leader's denial costs him nothing and yours still costs you everything.

What Actually Trades

  • Cash: The only thing that changes hands freely, off the board, any time. The grease in every deal below.
  • Liquor: By Move; barrels left in a rival's District become theirs. The rulebook calls it a donation; it is also the sanctioned way to pay in product, and a barrel of Rum is $300–$500 and a Kickback, the richest coin at the table.
  • The Mash: The Harbormaster names tomorrow's number for the whole city, and a face that fires an ally's boilers as well as his own costs him nothing. The cheapest favour in the game, and the easiest to collect on later.
  • The Market: A walk-away that frees a card, a claim that denies one, a morning's pick conceded. Priced in mornings, the scarcest currency there is.
  • The queue: Stepping off early or hanging on late to hand an ally the token he needs. Free to give whenever your own evening didn't matter.

And one thing never trades: Influence. Your markers fund your Plays, stake your Jobs and buy your crown, and the game gives you no way to slide one across the table. Every deal above exists because that one doesn't: when tempo itself can't be lent, everything else becomes the currency.

The Handshake: Reputation and Sabotage

When a deal needs teeth, you shake on it where the table can see. Terms must be clear, and every deal dies at the end of the current Day. Kept: the deal simply completes, and both reputations walk away clean. Broken, for any reason, including somebody else's interference: the breaker takes a Welsher card, and it is −1 Respect when the books open, forever. The cards stack, and nothing in the rules removes one. A handshake costs nothing to offer and nothing to keep, which makes it the cheapest instrument at the table and the most expensive one to misuse. Four consequences follow, and only the first one is friendly:

  • Promise only what you alone control. "I will not cross the river today" cannot be sabotaged: the only person who can break it is you. "You'll cross my Borough unharmed" is a promise about the whole table's behaviour, and any third crew can make a welsher of you with one Move. Word your deals as your own actions, never as outcomes, and read every deal you're offered the same way: who besides him can break this?
  • A rival's handshake is a target. The card falls on the promiser however the terms fail, so the moment two rivals shake, sabotaging the deal joins your list of available Plays. Block the passage, shoot the man he promised to protect, rob the courier carrying the payment. One Move of yours, and a rival wears −1 Respect to the end of the game.
  • Price the point before you promise. A Welsher card is a permanent rung off the fifteen you need, and unlike turf or Titles it can never be won back. A boss at 14 Respect who breaks a deal (or has one broken for him) un-crowns himself on the spot; the same card ten Days from the finish is nearly free. If you must promise on the night you mean to win, promise small, promise early, and promise nothing a rival can touch.
  • And read whose word is cheap. Respect is fully public, so the price of a man's promise is always on the scoreboard: a boss one point off the crown will die keeping his word, and a boss ten points back, or one already wearing the Rat's −3, is selling paper the street has already discounted. Count his distance from fifteen before you count on his handshake, and count the Welshers already in his Respect pile: a serial breaker's word isn't collateral, it's confetti.

The Off-Book Sale: Arbitrage in Barrels

The third instrument the rules hand you is the plainest: cash changes hands off the board, any time, for anything. Most bosses spend it on a walk-away or safe passage. The sharper trade is product, but read the new economy first. Moonshine dumps at a flat $300 and never sees the inside of a swanky room; Havana Rum is the only bottle the $500 chandeliers will pour. Between the Still that brews the grey, the Dock that turns it Brown, and the High Society room that pays double for it, no one boss usually owns all three links. Every gap in that chain is a business, if you can find the man who holds the link you're missing.

The High Society arbitrage

Say a rival holds one of the four $500 rooms but works no water to fill it, and you run the Docks but own no premium address to pour into. You are two halves of one bottle. So you Trade your Moonshine up to Rum at your Dock, then Move the Rum into his District (Cost 1), and by the rulebook's own donation rule it becomes his the moment it lands. He Unloads it for $500 under the lights and pays you back more than the $300-and-a-Kickback your own Standard joint would ever have handed you. Read that ledger and it is the rarest thing at this table, an honest win for both chairs: you banked a premium your address could never pay, and he stocked a room he could never fill. The gap between $300 and $500 is real money, and it exists only because the chain is split between you.

The standing shop

Turn that one sale into a trade and post a price. “I'll pay top dollar for any barrel of Rum delivered to a room I control today.” Back the words with a Handshake so the table knows the money is real, and let the whole city run your distribution: every crew that works a Dock but owns no premium room now has somewhere to send its Rum, and every barrel that arrives you sell on at $500. Two cautions, and they are the whole of Parts I and IV in a breath. First, Rum is a 15-barrel pool: a shop that hoards it corners the entire city's premium trade, which is exactly why rivals will burn to break it. Second, a pile of barrels is a target. The Raid kicks the fattest-barrelled door in reach, so a shop that fills on the wrong afternoon is a decoy warehouse you built for the cops, by accident. Run it, but keep your Safehouse and your Boss on another block, and know the mornings your own name sits freshest on the Track.

The Rum premium

One thing rides along with every Rum sale that no cash price captures. A barrel of Havana Rum pays whoever Unloads it the cash plus a Kickback, that late-day marker from Part II that hands a boss his afternoon back. So when you Move Rum to a partner's room you are selling him two things at once: a $500 sale and a Play, and you charge for both. The premium is yours to name, and he still comes out ahead, because the Kickback he buys off you is worth more to a boss running dry at dusk than the coins it cost him, and never less than the $100 Odd Jobs pays for it on his way off the street. The richest coin at the table, changing hands at a price the coin itself sets.

Conspiracies: Aiming the Table at One Man

Every deal above is two crews splitting a profit. The deadlier ones split a victim. When the board hands you and a rival the same enemy, the cheapest Play on the board is often the one you make on his behalf.

Mutual targets: back his war for your prize

Two rivals lock horns over a Borough you don't want. Read past the Borough. If the man on the defensive holds a Dock, and losing it would tip the Harbormaster's crown onto your head (most Docks, Part I), then his war is your business. Post the attacker a bounty: “$1,000 if that Dock changes hands today.” Cash is free to promise and pays only on results, and you're backing a battle whose spoils you never touch: he takes the turf, you take back the dice. The trick is to want a different prize than the men doing the bleeding. They fight for the ground; you fight for what the ground controls.

Catching the leader: the cartel of denial, in moves

Part II says the crown is denied at the Jobs Market, and no single rival can afford that denial twice. Here is what the cartel actually does, once three bosses agree the leader has run far enough:

  • Leave his number in the pool. Conspire to forgo a Red you'd each have liked, so the leader's firing face sits in the pool as the Blowback and bursts the boiler he parked his Boss on. Nobody fired a shot; the dice did it, because three men agreed not to draft.
  • Rent the last man's morning. By the Reverse Snake (Part II) the last boss off the street picks the Market first, ahead of the leader. When that morning isn't yours, it's for sale: a cash bounty for lifting the crowning card out from under the leader turns your problem into another man's payday. He spends the stake and wears the denial, you pay the rent, and the leader watches the card walk.
  • Pay a rival to strike the match. A cash bounty rents another crew's signature Play against the leader: hire the Knights to Torch his Safehouse (his only Recruit anchor), or the Sicilians to put a Hit on his Boss. Off-book cash is free to promise, and the man you pay wears the Heat, not you.
  • Corner him, then call. Pay a rival to plant a Runner across the leader's one open exit, then play the Rat. A raided crew with no Land Connected Safe District is not fined, it is boxed (Part IV). You bought the lock; the law kicks the door.
  • Sell his Rum back to him. If the leader is Rum-rich and High-Society-poor, let him Move his Havana Rum into a $500 room you control, Unload it under your lights, and split the premium. He turns product into Respect's fuel; you skim the difference, hold the only door his good stuff can walk through, and read the shape of his plan off what he asks you to sell.

Put out a hit. The purest conspiracy needs no partner, only a wallet. When you and a rival are racing for the same Offer, don't out-run him, un-man him: post a bounty the whole table can collect. “$1,000 to whoever kills the Irish Boss today.” A crew with no Boss on the board skips The Offers the next morning (Part II), so the card you both wanted falls to you uncontested, and the killing was somebody else's gun, somebody else's Heat, and your money. The bounty is free to name; you only pay if it works. Post it late, though: you have just told the man to spend two Influence Rising the old don into the safest block he owns, and a Boss who moves before the guns arrive costs you nothing but the announcement.

The Frame-Up: Selling the Raid

Part IV taught you to aim the law at your own decoy. The same aim works on a rival, and a deal is how you take it. The Raid kicks the fattest-barrelled door in each Squad's reach, so the man with the biggest stockpile chooses nothing and loses everything, and where the barrels sit is a thing you can arrange.

  • Frame a rival. Before you drop a dime, donate barrels onto a rival's block, fattening his District into the obvious target. Then Rat, or simply wait for the fifth marker to land: the cops walk past your empire and kick the door you stocked for them.
  • The double cross. Move him liquor to sell, shake on it, and drop the dime the same afternoon. He masses the barrels for tomorrow's sale exactly as you asked; the Raid reads that pile as a confession, and you watch the cops break down a door you loaded. The Handshake even leaves you looking like a partner, right up to the sirens.
  • The liquor defense. Run it backwards to guard your own turf. When a rival covets a District of yours and the Track runs hot, leave barrels on it and Hold Fire when he comes. He shoots first, so he draws the Heat in the taking (the freshest marker on the Track) and inherits the stockpile he just captured. One phone call later he is the loudest, richest man in reach: caught red-handed, holding your barrels, his own name freshest on the sheet. The turf you “lost” was the bait.

Specialist Services: Renting the Signature Play

Every family owns two Plays no rival can copy, and a monopoly is a shop. What your crew can do and theirs cannot, they will pay to rent, because the alternative is not for sale at any price.

  • The Sicilians sell cool. Only Consigliere lifts a marker off the Heat Track and hands it back to its owner's Reserves. To a rival cooking loud and dreading the sirens, that one phone call is worth real money, and the Syndicate is the only number that answers it.
  • The Knights sell fire and water. Their Torch is the cheapest way in the city to un-house a rival's Safehouse, and their Skiff runs five barrels clean across the water in a single Play. Sell the Torch as a weapon aimed at a third crew, and the Skiff as a freight line, the Staten lane on demand. The Network was always a business; the customers are just other bosses now.

Price every one of these the way the loan sharks price a debt. You are not being generous; you are the sole wholesaler of a thing your buyer cannot make, and the seams of this book, the moments where one chapter pays for another, are exactly where that thing is dearest.

The Blood Oath: Politics with a Deadline

The team variant turns everything above up to a boil, because the Federal Crackdown Tracker puts a date on the politics: every Raid advances it, and at space 10 the table freezes, ranks by Respect, and marries 1st to 4th, 2nd to 3rd, permanently. Play toward that moment, not just through it:

  • Your rank at the Sit-Down is a position, and you can play it. First place buys the weakest partner in the game; second place buys the balanced marriage. With the token on 8 or 9, a claim-late boss can sandbag a completed-Job-in-waiting for a morning (or sprint one home) to choose which wedding he attends. Both families race for the same 30, so every point you carry in is a point off the run: rank buys you a partner, but the Respect on your books buys you a head start. What you never get back is whose engine you're chained to, and whose home borough borders yours.
  • The clock itself is playable. Raids advance it, and Part IV taught you to fire Raids on demand. A bottom-of-the-table boss who Rats is not just calling the law: he's dragging the wedding forward while he's still cheap enough to marry the leader. A leader enjoying the solo game keeps the city cool for exactly the same reason. And the brand is on the guest list now: the Sit-Down ranks on current Respect, so the Card in your hand at space 10 can drop you a place and change who you marry. Ratting your way to the wedding while wearing it is how you make sure you are the cheap fourth the leader has no choice but to take.
  • Watch the honor you're marrying. No debtor can be crowned, and in the Blood Oath, neither can their Alliance. That fourth-place crew with two of Shylock's Marks isn't a bargain partner; he's a padlock on your throne until he's clean. The Sit-Down doesn't launder anybody, but the padlock has a price list, $2,000 a Mark, and cash crosses the table freely. Budget your partner's debts the way you'd budget your own. His Rat Card is a different animal: it doesn't bar the Alliance, it just quietly bills it 3 Respect on the night you were counting on his half of the total. Cheaper than a Mark, and far easier to forget.
  • After the oath, the family runs on cash and cargo. Influence never crosses the table, partner or not, so the engine of the variant is everything else this chapter prices: off-book cash funds the Bribes of whichever crew has the cheap Respect in front of it, and Havana Rum Moved into a partner's High Society room is the closest thing the game has to a legal tempo transfer, a $500 sale with a Kickback riding on it. The fine print of turf survives the wedding, too: his District is still Hostile ground, and his barrels are still his. Blood is thicker than water. It is not thicker than the rules.
  • And the family has one throne. The Capo di Tutti Capi is whoever contributed the most Respect to the winning total. The variant's last joke is that the race never ends: help your partner exactly enough to win, and not one Job more.
Kingpin's Note

In this business your word is the only collateral, and a handshake is a knife you hand a stranger handle-first. The greenhorn keeps his word out of decency and wonders why it keeps costing him. The old hand keeps it because the street keeps books. The made man writes terms only he can break, prices every promise against its breaker's distance from fifteen; and when two rivals shake hands across the table, he starts pricing what it would cost to make one of them a welsher. There are no shared victories in this city. There are only bills, and better company to split them with.

The Whole Day at Once

Five chapters, one machine. Watch a single Day through all five lenses and the seams show: at dawn you choose a number, and the docks choose whether you meant it. In the morning you reach for a card, or pointedly don't, and four people read the difference. All day you spend tempo (on turf, on volleys, on one more hour in the staring contest) while the law bills the noise to the only account you've got. And at nightfall the queue you fell into prices tomorrow: dice for the committed, cards for the free.

Three Questions Before Every Play

Which clock does this advance? The cheap Respect draining out of the deck, the ladder from six to ten, or the slow boil of the Track. A Play that advances none of them is a Play a rival would pay you to make. What does it tell the table? Your Jobs, your Respect, your stake, your barrels and your Heat are all public; your intention is the only secret you own, and most Plays spend a little of it. And who hears it? Every noise has a bill, and the bill has a name on it: make sure it's the right name, even if it isn't yours.
