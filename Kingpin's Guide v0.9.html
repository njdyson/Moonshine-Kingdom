THE KINGPIN'S GUIDE

The Arithmetic, the Angles, and the Applied Treachery.

The Rulebook tells you what you may do. This book is about what you should do, and to whom. Inside is the collected arithmetic of the whole racket: the dawn stills, the Jobs Market, the standoffs, the sirens, and the delicate business of trusting people who are paid to betray you, all of it measured over a few hundred thousand simulated mornings and written down in the plain talk of the street. Read it before your rivals do, or learn each chapter the way the city prefers to teach: at full price.

The Whole Racket

This is five guides in one, because Moonshine Kingdom is five games in one, played simultaneously, with the same ten markers. A boss who plays them separately is playing all five badly. The point of binding them into one book is the seams: every chapter ends where another begins, and the games are won in the seams.

The Day, in One Breath

Each Day runs dawn to midnight. Shadows: The city cooks a common mash, the boilers fire or burst, each boss takes his pick of the Jobs Market, and whatever Influence isn't promised elsewhere funds the day's Ledger. The Hustle: Plays around the table, one at a time, until every boss has stepped off the street, and the order they step off decides everything about tomorrow. Reckoning: The cops thin the armies, the Deeds and crowns change hands, and the city checks whether anyone just won.

The One Line That Runs the City

Plays today  =  Influence  −  staked  −  Heat,  up to the Ledger's 5 slots.

Commit that to memory, because every chapter in this book is a fight over one of its three terms. Influence is capacity, not actions: the Ledger stops at five slots, so the markers above your fifth are the ones that stake Jobs and soak up Heat for free. Staked is every promise you've made to the Market and not yet kept. Heat is every noise you've made that the law hasn't forgiven yet, and the nightly Reckoning does not forgive it; only a Raid does. Raise the first term, time the second, aim the third. That is the entire game, and the rest of this book is the detail.

The Three Clocks

Under the day loop, three slower clocks tick. The cheap Respect drains one way: completed Jobs leave the deck for good, and the easy tier goes first. The Influence ladder climbs one way: from the 6 markers everyone starts with to the 10 that crown a king, at $2,500 a rung. And the Heat cycle turns over and over: quiet, loud, sirens, refund, quiet. Every plan in this book is really a claim about which clock matters most right now.

A Note on the Figures

The combat and brewing numbers are Monte Carlo estimates from the game's own simulators, 100,000+ trials per figure, rules-accurate down to each mob's signature Plays. The Jobs and Heat numbers are deterministic: read straight off the rules and machine-checked against the live card files. What none of them can model is the table: who reads whose barrels, who blinks in the staring contest, and whether the man holding the card you need can be talked out of it. Those decide more games than the arithmetic does. Hence Part V.

Part I · The Brew Engine

Before the sun clears the tenements, the stills are already breathing. Steam in the pipes, mash in the boilers, and out the back a river of clear liquor that turns into a river of dirty cash. It looks like luck: a roll of the bones and a prayer. It isn't. Behind the dice sits a small, sharp piece of arithmetic, and the bosses who read it right out-earn the ones who don't without ever rolling a better number.

The One Rule That Governs Everything

Every dawn the city cooks a common mash. That's the Mash die (grey as the Moonshine it makes), sitting in the open for all to see. Then, in Turn Order, each boss drafts one Red die from the pool and lays it beside the Mash. The two together are your Brew Number, and that single number is the only tune your empire plays today. Every Still you Control whose boiler shows that number roars to life at once; every Still on a different number sits cold, a dead kettle in a dark room.

Read that twice, because the whole trade falls out of it: you only ever hit one number a day. A boss with a 7-boiler and an 8-boiler never lights both on the same morning: one cooks, the other waits its turn. But a boss with two 7-boilers fires both together, off the same lucky pair of dice. Matching numbers stack their yield; mismatched numbers take turns and leave you idle. The whole game of the Brew is deciding which numbers to marry your money to.

And here's the gift: you see tomorrow coming. The Mash for the next dawn is set the night before, at the close of the Shadows, and it sits face-up on the board through the entire day's business. So half of every Brew Number on tomorrow's table, and half of every Blowback that could gut your crew, is public knowledge a full day early. Seizing the Stills that pair with it, walking your Boss out of harm's way, Laying Low early to grab the first draw of the bones: those aren't hunches. They're the sharpest plays on the board, and the intel is already in your hand.

The Hand on the Dice: The Harbormaster

Here is the wrinkle that separates a kingpin from a gambler. That common mash doesn't fall from heaven. It comes ashore in barrels of molasses, up the piers, over the docks; and whoever holds the Harbormaster Title, the crown for Controlling the most Dock Districts, owns that trade. So at the close of each Shadows, the Harbormaster doesn't leave tomorrow's Mash to the roll of a die. He names it. Any face he likes, 1 through 6. Every drop of molasses lands on his wharves, and he decides what the whole town brews at dawn.

That turns half of every rival's Brew Number into a political number: a favour granted or a knife slipped in, depending on whose Stills he means to feed. He will name the face that lights his own boilers and leaves yours stone cold, and because the Mash sits in the open all day, the whole table watches the squeeze coming and can do nothing about it after the fact. The only question left worth asking is the one that wins games: what will he pick, and can he lock me out of my own morning?

The Magic of Seven

The answer to "can they lock me out?" comes down to a single, beautiful quirk of arithmetic. A boiler on number n can only fire if the Mash leaves a draftable Red (1–6) to complete it: that is, if n minus the Mash lands between 1 and 6. Work that through for every boiler number and a clear winner falls out:

On this board, "just cook sevens" isn't a hunch a made man passes down for luck. It's armour.

So when this chapter says "clump," understand what the board is actually offering: a wall of 8s or 9s isn't merely starvable, it's two boilers. The fat matching stack every boss dreams about exists on exactly one number, and it is the one number the docks can't starve. The catch, and it's a cruel one. There are only four 7-boilers in the whole city, and every last one sits under a High Society address, one to a mainland Borough, none out on Staten Island. Those are the $500 rooms, and they open the game padlocked by the Police until a Raid kicks the doors in (see Part IV). So a lockout-proof engine of sevens means prising cop-guarded, silk-lined turf out of three or four separate Boroughs at once: blood for every boiler. And to deny a rival the Mash on top of that, you'd have to wear the Harbormaster's crown yourself: the most Dock Districts, strung out across the water on the far shore. No single mob can grip a fat wall of sevens and the docks both; the crew stretches until it snaps. You choose a lane. Own the sevens, or own the piers. Almost no one owns both.

The Other Way Out: Spread the Ends

Can't fight your way to the sevens? There's a second road to immunity, and it runs against every instinct that says "clump." Instead of piling onto one number, bracket a low boiler and a high one. A pair like 3 and 9 can never be starved together: the face that kills the 3 (a high Mash) is the very face that feeds the 9, and the other way round. There is no single number the Harbormaster can name that darkens both ends at once. Squeeze one, you open the other.

It costs you something. A spread only ever lights one end a morning, so it never pours like a fat matching stack in a good week; but it always pours something, every single dawn, no matter whose crown sits on the docks. When a hostile Harbormaster is starving the table, that reliability is worth real money, as the ledgers below show. One thing to mind: the low numbers are scarce on the map, just two 2-boilers and two 3-boilers in the whole city, so you bracket the ends. You don't clump them.

Read the Docks: The Crown Can't Starve Itself

For all that dread, the Harbormaster wears a collar of his own making. He cooks Moonshine too (the same common mash the whole city brews), and the face he names lights his boilers exactly as it darkens yours. He cannot reach for a number that starves your Stills without first making sure it won't starve his own. His turf is a leash on the number he can pick, and a boss who reads that leash can walk right up to the crown and pick his pocket. Two things fall out of that, and both are yours to exploit:

  • You can predict tomorrow's Mash by reading the Harbormaster's Stills. A crew cooking high boilers (8s, 9s) will name a mid-to-high face to fire them; they daren't name a low one, because a low Mash leaves their own high Stills cold. Their turf tells you their hand a full Day early.
  • Mirroring is a hard lock. Build your Stills on the same numbers the Harbormaster holds. Whatever face he names to fire his own boilers fires yours too: there is no Mash he can pick that lights his engine and darks a matching one. He can only starve you by starving himself, and a boss who cooks Moonshine won't. Ride the crown's own greed.

This is why the truly spiteful shutout (the 8-stack starved to $0 in the ledgers below) only lands on a crew whose numbers don't overlap the Harbormaster's. Match his range and the worst case softens into "we both brew, every morning." The lesson isn't to fear the docks; it's to watch them.

The Kill Shot: Mirror, Then Lay Low First

Mirroring gets you a seat at the Harbormaster's table. Laying Low first takes his chair. The Harbormaster sets the Mash at the end of Shadows, in the open, before anyone acts. Then, all through the Hustle, tomorrow's Turn Tokens are claimed one by one in the order players Lay Low: step off the board first and you claim Token #1, the first draft of tomorrow's Red pool. He names, you read, and you choose whether to cut your day short for first pick tomorrow.

Here's why that's murder against a tight-clumped crown. When the Harbormaster cooks a single number, the face he names to fire it leaves exactly one firing Red in the whole pool: name Mash 2 to light your 8s and only a 6 completes them. That scarcity is the trap he laid for the table. But with first draft, you take that one golden Red. He built the shortage; you pocket the only key.

And The Offers run in reverse Turn Order (see Part II), so Token #1 picks the Jobs Market last: the chair costs today's tempo and tomorrow's pick of the work. Against a crown clumped on one number, 60% against 11% is worth almost any fare; against a spreading one, take the high token: pick the Jobs, and hold the match.

Three Principles of the Brew

1

Clump on a number they can't take from you

Boilers that share a number fire as one, so clumping is how you brew big; but which number you clump on matters as much as how many, and the board decides both. A wall of sevens is Harbormaster-proof, and it is also the only wall on the map: seven is the one number with four boilers. A "wall" of 8s or 9s is two kettles and a light switch the docks can flip off at dawn. So clump, by all means, but understand that clumping past a pair means 7 (four), 4 or 6 (three), and nothing else in the city. Cook seven, or make your peace with a rival who can leave your kettles cold whenever it suits him.

2

Spread your crew, don't pile it on

The Muscle Ratio is a staircase, not a ladder, and the Sweep decides which step you can stand on. Every Reckoning thins any District above 5 Mobsters back down to 5, and the Brew comes round the very next dawn, so 5 is all you will ever have on a boiler at first light. Five men brew 3 barrels. So does a sixth, if you could keep him, which is the whole point: the staircase pays nothing for the extra body, and the Sweep takes him anyway. Five men to a Still is the sweet spot. Truth is, only the odd men earn at all: a growing crew brews 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, so the second man and the fourth carry nothing but their own thirst. Crew in odd numbers and you are never paying a wage for a barrel you don't get.

That puts a hard roof on the morning: 3 barrels a boiler, 4 with the Boss stood on it, and no crew in this city can push past it, save one. The Sicilians are Untouchable: the Sweep never thins their ranks, so they alone can wall 9 men onto a boiler, climb to the cap of 5, and Twist the Valves to 6. Everyone else buys breadth, because depth is not for sale, and that is no hardship. Once a boiler has its five, the next man you hire earns far more opening a second matching boiler, which fires the same morning, off the same pair of dice. Breadth of fire beats depth on one.

3

Fight for the docks, or mirror the man who holds them

The Harbormaster's crown is a hand on every rival's throat, but it has two soft spots. First, it's hard to keep: Eight Docks, strung across the water and all connected as if one, so no runaway holds the whole waterfront for long. Take the piers and you take back the dice. Second, if you can't take them, mirror them, then Lay Low first: Build on the crown's own numbers, beat him to the first draw, and lift the one Red his own Mash left in the pool. Grievance is the only play that never works. The docks answer to force or to imitation, never to complaint.

Park the Boss: Twist the Valves

One lever the tables leave out: any Still that fires with your Boss standing on it brews +1 barrel. Since your matching boilers all fire off the same Brew Number, park the old man on the stack and pocket a free barrel every morning it lights up. But there's a knife to it: unlike a firefight, a Blowback plays no favourites, and if your number is the one left to burn, the Boss is the first body on the floor; and a crew with no Boss on the board skips The Offers the next morning, so your rivals pick over the Market without you while you spend a Play on a Rise.

Here is the good news, and it costs you nothing: your seat doesn't matter, until your number doubles. Whatever chair you draft from, the Red you take leaves the pool with you. If the face you need showed up once in the roll, you have already carried it off the table, and no leftover can ever burn that boiler. A clump on one number is only ever in danger from a double: two 4s on a Mash of 3, you lift one, and a rival cheerfully leaves you the other. Your 7 fires and your 7 burns off the same morning's dice. That is when the chair matters, and only one chair helps: the last seat picks from the final two dice, so he alone decides what stays on the table. A Boss brewing is a Boss earning; a Boss brewing on a doubled number, from any chair but the last, is a Boss gambling with his life.

The real danger isn't the seat, it's the shape of your build. Count how many of your boiler numbers are reachable this morning: each one is another face that can be left behind to gut you, including boilers you never brewed from. A clump on a single number gives the table exactly one face to hurt you with. A 6/7/8 build, on a Mash of 3, is live on Reds 3, 4 and 5 at once: three different leftovers, any one of which bursts something of yours. The ledgers say it plainly: a wall of sevens loses its Boss on 7.6% of mornings; the 6/7/8 on 12.7%, near enough double, for the crime of cooking three numbers at once. And the spread? 2.5%. The ends of a true spread sit six apart, so they can never both be live in the same morning. The spread doesn't just dodge the Harbormaster. It's the safest place in the city to stand your Don.

Kingpin's Note

The greenhorn scatters his Stills to "cover more numbers," lights one boiler a day, and feeds the rest to the Blowback. The old hand cooks one number and gets rich. The made man does one better: he cooks a number the docks can't take from him, or he brackets the ends so no crown can leave him dry. And when a rival's Harbormaster turns greedy? He doesn't grumble: he cooks the crown's own numbers, Lays Low ahead of him for the first draw, and lifts the one Red the whole city's been starved of. The Harbormaster set the table. The made man eats first.

Part II · The Jobs Market

Deeds and Titles pay you for what you hold. Jobs pay you for what you do. Five cards lie face-up beside the board, in the open, every one of them a public dare, and none of them ever expires. There is no deadline in this business and no paperwork: only a stake, sitting on the table where everyone can count it, quietly costing you a Play a day for as long as your nerve holds out. Most bosses treat the Market like a shop. It isn't. It's a queue, a clock, and a confession, all at once, and the arithmetic underneath it decides more games than the dice ever will.

The One Rule That Governs Everything

A Job prints two numbers. Its Respect is what it pays. Its Stake is the Influence you lock out of your Reserves the morning you claim it, and don't get back until you pull it off. That's the whole contract. But read what a locked marker actually is, because the card doesn't tell you: The Offers run before Fund the Ledger. Stake first, fund second. Every marker sitting on a Job is a marker that never reaches your Ledger, and only Ledger markers buy Plays. So the stake is not a fee. It is rent, and the rent is paid in Plays, every single Day you hold the card. Claim a 5-Respect Job on Monday and pull it off on Thursday, and you have quietly handed your rivals four short days on the street. Nobody stops you. Nobody has to.

The whole system is the one line of arithmetic from the front of this book, Plays today = Influence − staked − Heat, up to five slots, and note what it doesn't say. There is no gate: no Job in this deck is ever unclaimable for want of markers. A big stake costs you actions, not access; you can always reach for the card, you simply buy it with your day. The trade is smooth, one marker for one Play, all the way down to a one-Play morning. Which makes the fifth slot the only thing resembling a cliff, because past it the trade stops costing anything at all.

Heat Is Billed to the Same Account

Now put the third term back, because that table is a fiction and every real morning has Heat in it. A noisy Play doesn't return its marker to your Reserves; it locks it onto the communal Heat Track, where it sits until a Raid clears the board. It is gone from the same pool the stake comes out of, and it is gone across Days: the Reckoning does not clear Heat. Only the sirens do. So the ladder in that table slides down one rung for every marker cooling on the Track. Your working capital is Influence minus Heat, and only the first five of it buys Plays. Two markers hot, and a 6-Influence crew has no free capacity at all: even a 1-Respect card starts costing a Play.

Now count the deck: fourteen of the thirty-two Jobs draw Heat in the act of completing them. All eight Open Fires, the five big Unloads, the lone Extort. The Market sells you cards that burn the very currency you need to claim the next one, and a crew halfway up the Heat Track has no room left to promise anybody anything. That is not an accident. There are only three answers: keep your Jobs quiet, climb the Influence ladder faster than you climb the Heat Track, or let the Raid come, because a Raid returns every Heat marker to its owner's Reserves. The cops kick your door in and refund your stake money on the way out (see Part IV).

Bribe is a Jobs play. Everyone reads Bribe as the last chore before the crown: four payments of $2,500 you make when you're already winning. It's the opposite. The 7th marker makes a 3-Respect Job free to hold; the 8th makes a 5 free; and every marker past that is a rung of headroom against the Heat you'll be generating to complete them. Those markers are the cheapest tempo in the game, they were on the road to the throne anyway, and the boss who buys them on Day 4 plays a different game from the boss who buys them on Day 12. The ladder isn't there to make Jobs free. It's there to keep them free once you're loud.

One Claim a Day Is a Rate, Not a Limit

Nothing caps how many Jobs you hold. The Offers let you claim one card a morning, and there is no deadline on any of them, so a patient boss simply accumulates: a 1 on Tuesday, a 3 on Thursday, a 5 the following week, all three sitting staked in front of him at once. The only ceiling is what your Reserves can carry, and the arithmetic runs on your total staked, not on the newest card. That changes the shape of a plan: a three-card overlap isn't a thing you assemble in an afternoon; it's three separate mornings, and the first card starts paying rent from the first one. But it also means the cheap tier compounds beautifully: two or three 1-Respect Jobs, all riding Plays your liquor business was making anyway, cost a total Stake of 3 and complete whenever the barrels happen to line up. Hold a small standing portfolio and let the board complete it for you.

The Five-Play Ceiling Is a Fiction: The Kickback

One rule breaks the whole funding equation open, and it's easy to file under "money" and forget. Every barrel of Havana Rum you Unload moves one spent Influence marker from your Reserves back into an empty Ledger slot. Not next morning: immediately, mid-Hustle. Moonshine pays cash only; Rum pays cash and gives you your day back. Two limits keep it honest: you can only pull back markers you have already spent this Day, and you can't overfill five slots, so the Kickback tops out at +3 Plays however deep the Rum runs. Both also mean the Kickback is a late-day engine: it does nothing at dawn and everything at dusk, exactly when your Ledger is running dry and the table is deciding who steps off first. Which is where it turns from an economic rule into a political one.

Sell late, and sell in fives. Two corollaries fall straight out of those limits, and both cost bosses money nightly. First, your empty slots are your Kickback capacity: a marker can only come home to a slot that's open, so Unload after you've spent your other markers, when the Ledger sits emptiest, never at dawn with all five slots full and nothing to recycle into. Second, six-plus Rum in a single Unload throws the recycling away: past the fifth barrel there is no slot left to fill, so the surplus sells for cash and nothing more. Pour a fat load all at once and you've handed back the very trait you paid a Dock to earn; hold the extra Rum for another emptied Ledger, and it keeps buying your day back one sale at a time.

The Market Never Moves, and That's the Whole Design

Nothing on the street ages. No card slides down a row, nothing goes stale, nothing is swept away at dawn. The Market changes only when a player changes it: somebody takes a Job or walks away from one, and a fresh card fills the gap. That single fact rewrites how you're supposed to play.

First: there is no clock but your own money. Since the card cannot expire, claiming it early buys you exactly one thing (certainty that a rival won't take it), and you pay for that certainty in Plays, daily, until you fire. Which produces the doctrine this whole chapter is built on:

The Claim-Late Doctrine

Every Job in the deck completes in a single Play. So build the position first, in silence, with your Reserves full and your Ledger fat, and claim the card on the morning you can fire it that same afternoon. One Day of rent, full Respect. A 5 taken and finished on the same Day costs you two Plays. The same 5 claimed three Days early costs you six.

Second: the Market lets you plan in silence, and the tell is your board, not your hand. Because the card will still be there next week, the correct play against a card you want is often to say nothing and move barrels. But understand what you're hiding and what you're not. The Job is public. The stake is public. Your intention is the only secret you own, and it leaks through the board, not the Market. Six barrels drifting toward Fordham, a Safehouse creeping into a rival's Ward, your Boss walking into Queens: a boss who reads turf can see a claim coming a full Day before you make it, and take the card out from under you.

Third: a named Job is a standing bounty. Under a churning market, the correct answer to Kill a rival Boss in Queens sitting face-up would be to wait for it to rot off the end of the row. The Staring Contest, and What It's Actually About

Late in the Hustle, with two markers left and nothing urgent to spend them on, you are in a staring contest. Step off now and you take the low token: tomorrow's dice, tomorrow's tempo, and a Market picked over by the time it reaches you. Burn a Play you don't strictly need and you outlast the man beside you for first refusal on the street. But tempo in the abstract is almost never what the contest is about. On most evenings nobody cares and everyone drifts off in whatever order their markers ran out. The contest sharpens on exactly one kind of night: when a card is face-up that two of you want, and both of you know it. Then the queue is the whole game, and it is won three ways: by reading him, by hiding yourself, and by having the Rum to outlast him.

Layer one: read what they're building toward

A rival's plan is written in two places, and neither of them is his face. His staked hand is public: every Job he holds is face-up, so you already know what he needs. His board tells you when he'll be ready for it: barrels drifting toward one Speakeasy, a Safehouse creeping off his home turf, bodies gathering two blocks from a Dock. Put the two together and you can date his claim to within a Day. Then do the same for the cards he hasn't claimed: the harder read and the more valuable one, because it lets you deny him something he hasn't paid for yet. A crew that has spent four Days massing Rum in Red Hook is telling you which card it wants off the street, whether or not that card is in its hand.

Layer two: don't be readable yourself

Everything above is being done to you. The Claim-Late Doctrine says build first and claim on the morning you fire, but that only works if the build is quiet, because the window in which you're vulnerable is precisely the window in which you're visibly ready and don't own the card yet.

  • Assemble late and assemble fast. Six barrels arriving in Fordham across four Days is an announcement. Six barrels arriving in one Move, on the morning you claim, is a fait accompli.
  • Don't let the last Play be the obvious one. If your position only makes sense as a run at one specific card, you have already told them. Stage from somewhere that threatens two things.
  • Weigh claiming early anyway. Sometimes the tell is unavoidable: you cannot hide a Safehouse walking across the map. When the build is inherently loud, pay the rent and lock the card, because the alternative is doing the work for someone else.
  • Or be the Vipers. Whispers claims the deck's top card face-down. Rivals see a Stake and nothing else: the only way in the game to hold a Job that cannot be read, denied, or pre-empted.

Layer three: Rum wins the staring contest

And here is the lever, the one that turns the whole thing from a guessing game into a plan. Everyone else's day ends when their Ledger runs dry. A crew holding Rum decides when its day ends. Sell three barrels and you are a Play up, with no Heat; sell four and you're two up. So when a card you both want is sitting on the street, you do not have to hope he blinks: you Unload, refill your Ledger, and simply outlast him, then take first pick at dawn. That is what makes Rum the quiet backbone of a Jobs game. It pays the same cash as Moonshine, so a crew that never Trades sees no difference on the ledger sheet and cannot understand why it keeps getting to the Market last. And it compounds three ways at once: the Rum ladder Jobs (The Angel's Share, Cuban Prince, High Roller, The Smuggler's Run) pay Respect for the very sales that buy your tempo, the Knights get a free barrel on every Trade, and a Rum sale at a High Society joint pays $500 a barrel on top. Trade up, or spend the game explaining why the good cards keep going to somebody else.

When to blink first anyway

  • Step off early when tomorrow's Mash is a number you own, when the pool looks likely to leave a Blowback on your own boiler, or when there is simply nothing on the street you could complete this week. Dice beat cards when the cards don't fit you.
  • Hang on to the end when a card you can fire tomorrow is face-up, when a rival is visibly one Play from a Job you both want, or when you're the Harbormaster and named tomorrow's Mash yourself: you already know what the dice will do, so spend the token on the Market instead.
  • Remember Odd Jobs. Stepping off with markers still in the Ledger pays $100 apiece. It's the consolation prize for losing the contest, and on a Day you're saving toward a Bribe it's reason enough to blink first: the Bribe buys you the queue back permanently.

No Boss, no business. A crew with no Boss on the board skips The Offers entirely: no claim, no walk away, nothing, until they spend a Play to Rise. So killing a rival's Boss is not merely a Respect card and a −1 to his Threat: it deletes him from tomorrow's queue. If you and he are racing for the same card, the cheapest way to win the race is to shoot the man out of it. The Blowback will do it for free if he parks the old man on a doubled number, so watch the pool as closely as you watch the Market.

Logistics: the tier that pays you for playing well

Fifteen of thirty-two cards ask you to move, trade or sell liquor at scale, and if your Brew engine is working, you were going to do that anyway. The Angel's Share (3+ Rum at a $300 joint), Last Call (4+ barrels), The Dutchman's Deal (Trade 3+ at a Dock): these are 1-Respect cards that ride a Play already on your list, for a Stake of 1, which is free at the Influence everyone starts with. There is no cheaper Respect in the game and there never will be. Literally: completed Jobs leave the deck permanently, the 1s are the preferentially-claimed tier, and there are only twelve of them. The cheap Respect drains out of the game one way. A boss who sniffs at 1-Respect cards on Day 3 is a boss shopping in a Market of nothing but campaigns on Day 12.

Muscle: you don't have to win, you only have to kill

Read the rule that governs every combat Job, because it is more generous than it looks: a Job is a deed, not a board position; the deed counts even if it didn't survive the Play. Kill the man, then lose the block, and the Job still pays. The Pier Six Brawl, The Irish Goodbye, The Butcher's Ledger and The Toll Booth Trap ask for kills, not for ground. A suicide raid that murders two dockhands and gets thrown back into the river is a completed Job. Only the cards that say Seize, take over or destroy need the outcome to stick. What makes them hard is the arithmetic on the other side: a firefight counts as one Play however many rounds it runs, but every round costs its own Influence out of your Ledger, so the real ceiling on a combat Job is the Ledger's five slots. Stake 3 for a headline kill-count Job at 6 Influence and you walk in with three markers (one to enter, two to shoot) against a garrison Part III says needs three-to-one and four volleys to break.

Estate: the cards that move your whole game

Six cards ask you to put your Safehouse somewhere specific, and since Recruit only happens at your Safehouse, that is never a cosmetic request. Union Dues and Last One Standing both push the house into a Borough whose Deed you don't hold; The Beachhead puts it next door to a rival's; High Roller and Fortress Staten pick the district outright. Claim one of these and you are agreeing to rebuild your spawn point in somebody else's neighbourhood, which is a campaign, not a Play. Their compensation is that they're the only Respect in the deck that nobody can interrupt. No rival stands between you and your own Safehouse. If the board has turned hostile and the muscle cards are out of reach, the Estate family is where a losing position can still bank points.

The odd three, and the trap in each

  • The Empty Casket (Rise a new Boss in a Ward you Control) reads like a consolation prize for a disaster. It isn't; it's an insurance policy, and the difference is the timing. You cannot claim it after your Boss dies: no Boss means you skip The Offers entirely, and by the time you've Risen, the deed is behind you and the card is unclaimable forever. It only ever pays if you were already holding it when the worst happened. At Stake 1 it costs nothing to carry. Take it on a quiet morning, stake it, and forget it. Mind that it wants a Ward you Control, so keep one in the family.
  • The Insurance Job (Rat, and have the Raid padlock a low-Pressure Still you Control) hands you the Rat Card, which blocks Bribing and the crown until it leaves you. That makes it strictly an early-game card: 3 Respect while your Influence ladder hasn't started, and a self-inflicted wound afterwards. The engineering it wants is a Part IV masterclass: the Squad hunts the freshest name on the Track, then its most-barrelled district in reach, so the last shout in town must be yours, with the barrels piled on the boiler you want padlocked.
  • The Five Families (Extort from a District in all five Boroughs, one Play) is the deck's only Extort, and its price is Staten Island, the borough nobody visits. Five Respect for holding a footprint nobody else bothers to build. It always draws Heat.

Denial: Taking a Card You Never Intend to Do

You may claim any Job on the street. Nothing says you have to complete it. Take the card a rival has spent four Days building toward and it is simply gone: not discarded, not recycled, just locked in your hand for as long as you care to pay for it. This is real and it wins games. It is also priced, and you should know the bill before you reach. It costs your entire Offer, twice: Take and Walk Away are one of three, never two, so denying a card burns the morning you take it and a second morning to shed it. It costs the stake, every Day in between: at 6 Influence, sitting on a rival's 5 costs you two Plays a Day for as long as the grudge lasts. It costs the queue: a shorter Ledger walks you off the street early, so tomorrow the rival you just robbed picks before you. And it doesn't stop the deed: he can still do the thing, he just doesn't get paid for it. So below ten Influence, hate-drafting is a genuine sacrifice, reserved for the moment it decides the game: a rival on 12 or 13 Respect reaching for the card that crowns him. Then it is cheap at any price, because there is no tomorrow to save Plays for.

The one that isn't a sacrifice. A kingpin at 10 Influence has five markers with nothing else to do. He can take the deck's best card, hold it forever, never complete it, and lose nothing at all: his Ledger still fills, his day is still five Plays long, and his markers still count toward the crown wherever they sit. If the leader is at ten and the Market is thin, expect the good cards to start disappearing into a hand that will never play them. The counter is not to out-bid him; it is to get to ten yourself, or to end the game before he's comfortable there.

Three Principles of the Offers

1

Set up in silence; claim on the morning you fire

Every Job in the deck completes in a single Play, and nothing on the street ever expires. Those two facts together mean the stake is a rent you should almost always refuse to pay. Build the barrels, walk the Safehouse, mass the bodies; then take the card at The Offers and finish it that afternoon. One Day of rent instead of five. The only reasons to claim early are denial, or a rival visibly reaching for the same card; and if it's the latter, remember that the tell was never the Market. It was your board, and he was reading it three Days ago.

2

Climb the Influence ladder before you shop

Plays = Influence − staked − Heat, up to five slots. Nothing is ever unaffordable (a stake costs you actions, not access), but below the fifth slot every marker you promise or scorch is a Play you don't make. The markers above the fifth are the free ones, and they're bought with Bribes you were making anyway: staked markers still count toward your ten, wherever they sit on the night. There is no cheaper tempo in the game. And keep the account honest, because the third term is the one that ruins plans: Heat comes out of the same pocket, and the Reckoning doesn't clear it. Run the loud Jobs in bursts, keep quiet ones staked in between, and remember the Raid is a refund.

3

Know which half of the morning you're buying

The Brew drafts forwards and the Market picks backwards, so your Turn Token is a trade, not a rank. Lay Low first and you buy tomorrow's dice and tomorrow's tempo, and surrender the pick of the street. Lay Low last and you buy the Market, and take the leftover Red with the match in your hand. Neither is better; what's fatal is not choosing. And watch the loop that decides it for you if you don't: a heavy stake shortens your Ledger, a short Ledger walks you off the street early, and an early exit puts you at the back of tomorrow's queue. The Market taxes the man who's already been shopping. Two things buy you out of it: ten Influence, and Rum.

Kingpin's Note

The greenhorn grabs the biggest card on the street the day he sees it, pays two Plays a morning for a week, tells the whole table what he's building, and watches the target walk out of the borough. The old hand takes the small ones, the ones his liquor business was doing anyway, and quietly banks four Respect while the shooting goes on somewhere else. The made man does one better: he buys the Commission early, because at ten Influence a promise costs him nothing he'd have spent (so long as he keeps the Track cool); then he sets up in silence, claims on the morning he fires, and takes the last card off the street on the night the second-place man needed it. Nothing in the Market ever expires. Neither does a grudge.

Part III · The Standoff

Guns don't win this war; Respect does. But the turf that carries your Jobs, your Deeds, and your Speakeasies never comes quietly, and that makes a loaded pistol the down-payment on the crown. Here's the secret the corner boys never learn: most firefights are settled before a single die leaves the hand, in the long silence of the standoff, in the cold sum of who holds the better ground. Learn that ground, and you'll win wars you never have to fight.

Two Numbers Run Every Fight

Everything comes down to how many dice you roll and what kills. Dice = the Muscle Ratio, the same staircase as brewing: 1–2 mobsters roll 1 die, 3–4 roll 2, 5–6 roll 3, 7–8 roll 4, 9+ roll 5, the cap. More bodies, more lead, with hard diminishing returns. Threat = the kill number. Base kills on a 5–6; each +1 Threat widens it, to Level 4's 2+ (five-sixths of every die). Threat matters far more than dice, because it multiplies every die you roll. And Threat is where the defender lives: a dug-in Occupier stacks bonuses an attacker simply cannot match.

The Standoff: The Whole Game in Two Questions

When you Move onto turf a rival Controls, the fight pauses. First the Occupier answers; then the Invader chooses, and keeps choosing until the Pin breaks.

The Occupier answers first

Ambush

A free shot at +1 Threat before anyone else fires. No Heat. Then the fight is on.

Hold Fire

No shot. The Invader is Pinned but unhurt, a signal: "maybe you're just passing through."

Fold

Give up the block, flee your crew to safe turf. Lose the liquor, keep the bodies. Costs nothing.

Then the Invader chooses, and pays

Open Fire – 1 Influence

Both sides roll at once. If you kill, you take Heat. Still standing? Pay again to fire again.

Advance – 1 Influence

Shoot your way out to safe turf, barrels and all. A fighting retreat, not a rout.

Fall Back – free

Cut and run to where you came from, liquor left behind. Forced on you at 0 Influence.

The asymmetry that defines combat: The Occupier shoots first and free, adds +2 Threat from a Safehouse, and never takes Heat for defending his own corner. The Invader pays Influence for every volley and draws Heat the moment he kills. Defence is the strong position, by a wide margin. Attacking is a premium you pay in dice, in Influence, and in police attention.

Three Principles of the Street

1

Never attack into even numbers

The defender's free ambush shot and Safehouse +2 mean a fair fight isn't fair: it's a 1-in-50 for the attacker. If you mean to take a block, bring at least double the garrison, ideally into the next dice tier (5–6 bodies for 3 dice, 9 for the 5-dice cap). Anything less just donates your crew and your Heat to the man you're attacking.

2

The Influence tax caps your persistence

Every Open Fire costs 1 Influence: a Play you're not spending on money or Jobs. Grinding a stubborn alley from 3 volleys to 5 lifts your odds barely (25% → 31%) while your losses climb and rivals run free across the city. Commit hard or not at all: Plan the force to win in 2–3 volleys, and if it's not breaking, Fall Back before you're bled dry. There is no cheap war of attrition here.

3

Fold to save the crew; fight only for turf worth blood

Folding costs zero casualties: you trade the block and its barrels for a mobile, intact crew that lives to earn tomorrow. Standing to fight a stronger raider bleeds you whether you hold or not (2–3 casualties average even at a coin-flip). So fold the empty corner without a second thought; dig in only when your hold odds are high, or the block is worth a crown: a district your staked Jobs name, a Borough Deed, a Ward or Dock that swings a Title, or a Speakeasy your Night Mayor race can't spare.

Firepower is a free attack die on every Open Fire volley (strictly an invader's tool: it never fires on defence, not even an Ambush), and it's worth roughly a full dice tier. A concentrated assault of Boss and 6 Runners into a Secured 3-Runner block jumps from 48% to 79%; into a full garrison, it triples the take (9% → 29%). On their own turf the Irish defend like anybody else. Plunder is a different weapon: hit soft stockpiles. Against two unguarded Runners sitting on barrels, one quiet volley lifts ~1.3 barrels for 3 Influence, under one body lost, and zero Heat, the only violence in the city the law ignores. Against a Secured stockpile it trades ~3 bodies for a single barrel: don't. And loot only leaves on an Advance; Fall Back drops it.

Sicilian Syndicate: the wall and the stiletto

Everyone else's garrison is capped by the Sweep at 5 mobsters. Untouchable isn't: a Sicilian Boss + 6 Runners behind a Safehouse holds 80% against the biggest raid in the game (10 men, 4 Influence); Boss + 8 Runners holds 99%. Past nine defenders the attacker's five-dice cap simply cannot cut through Threat 4: it is a wall, and the only real check on it is that 15 Runners stretched over an empire leave every other border thin. Hit is the opposite tool: Move in, pay 2, and the rival Boss eats the first hit (68–94% he dies depending on your crew, even into a full garrison), then Fall Back. Three Influence, and the rival loses his +1 Threat everywhere on the map, plus 2 more Influence to Rise; and he skips tomorrow's Offers. You were never there for the turf.

East Side Vipers: the scalpel

Stealth deletes the Occupier's two best answers (no Ambush, no Fold), and it shows against thin turf: a five-man Stealth squad takes a Boss + 2 Runner + Safehouse block 70% of the time versus 24% marching in, at roughly half the casualties. But the 5-mobster cap means it cannot out-muscle a full garrison (7%, no better than walking). Snipe the thin blocks, the lone Bosses, the half-guarded Stills; never use it as a siege engine.

Harlem Knights: buy your wars

No Knight play adds dice or Threat: in a straight firefight they are the baseline row, everywhere, the only crew that fights bare-handed. What they do own is reach: Skiff lands five men from any coast you control onto any other, so an isolated picket anywhere on the water is prey. A full skiff takes two lone Runners 92% of the time for barely a body, and even a Boss with two guards falls near a coin-flip. No other crew delivers that fight without marching the map. Torch is the other lever and it's a siege tool: 3 Influence, a sacrificed Runner, and Heat to burn the Safehouse, which lifts tomorrow's assault from 7% to 18% and deletes the +2 Threat until the rival re-Secures ($500 and a Play). Prep, not takeover; or pure spite on the way out. The rest of the Knight war is economic: the rum trade fills the bankroll, the bankroll fills Recruit, and Recruit is how you show up with the three-to-one the tables above demand. Outnumber first; fight second.

So What's the Optimal Play?

  • Defending: A Safehouse plus 2–3 Runners and your Boss turns a district into a wall (66–90% hold vs. a real raid). Secure the turf that carries your win (the districts your Jobs name, Borough Deeds, the Wards, Docks and Speakeasies that swing your Titles) and let the scraps go.
  • Attacking: Only with overwhelming force (2–3× the garrison) into turf you genuinely need, and only when you can afford the Influence and the Heat. A raid that stalls is worse than a raid never launched.
  • Above both: Most of your combat value is positional: garrisons that deter, standoffs that bluff, folds that preserve. Spend bullets rarely and decisively; spend the threat of them constantly.

Part IV · Heat and the Law

Every chapter in this book keeps arriving at the same line of arithmetic, and the third term is the one nobody budgets for. The Heat Track is the only bank in the city where all four mobs keep their money in the same drawer: five spaces, filling one loud Play at a time, until the sirens empty it back into everyone's pockets at once. Most bosses treat it as weather. It isn't. It's a machine with a trigger, a targeting system, and a refund policy, and a boss who reads them can aim the law the way he aims a gun.

Only four things in this city put a marker on the Track, and the discounts written into them are the whole strategy:

Defending is free: the Occupier never draws Heat, whatever the bodycount; the law only books the man who started it, so the cheapest violence in the city is the violence somebody else begins on your doorstep. Plunder is free: the Irish steal barrels at gunpoint and the law shrugs. Move, Trade, Secure, Recruit, Bribe, and a disciplined three-barrel sale are all silent. A crew that wanted to could play an entire game without one marker reaching the Track, and fourteen of the thirty-two Jobs make sure it never wants to.

The principle underneath that table: the law counts Plays, not corpses. One firefight that kills five men costs one marker; five skirmishes that kill one man each cost five. One Extort squeezing eight districts costs one; and the 8-Rum Opening Night costs exactly what a 4-Rum sale costs. So when you must be loud, be loud in bulk: batch the noise into the fewest Plays the board allows, and bank the discount every time.

What Heat Actually Costs You

A marker on the Track came out of the same ten that fund your Ledger, stake your Jobs, and buy your crown, and it is not coming back at nightfall. The Reckoning does not clear Heat. Only a Raid does. So every hot marker is a rung off the ladder in Part II, this morning and every morning until the sirens. Two markers cooling and a 6-Influence crew can't carry a 1-Respect Job for free anymore; three, and their whole day is shorter than a kingpin's afternoon.

But note the one thing Heat does not cost you, because it changes the endgame: markers on the Track still count toward your ten. The win check counts your Influence wherever it sits: Reserves, Ledger, staked on Jobs, or cooling in a cell. The law taxes your days. It cannot touch your throne. A boss one Bribe from the crown does not need a cool Track; he needs a Reckoning where the count comes up ten, and the Track counts.

The Sicilian dial. Consigliere is the only Play in the game that turns the Track down: one Influence, one phone call, and any marker of the Syndicate's choosing goes home to its owner's Reserves. Read that again: any marker, anyone's. Cooling their own name is the obvious use. The other use is the one to fear, and it's in the Raid rules below: the law hunts the freshest marker, so removing the man in front of you from the queue re-aims the coming Raid at whoever stood behind him. The Syndicate doesn't just quiet the city. It decides who the city is loud about.

How the Raid Picks Its Door

The fifth marker lands, or somebody Rats, and the city locks down. What happens next is completely deterministic (no dice, no discretion), and that is the single most important fact about it, because anything deterministic can be engineered. Each of the four Squads, in the numbered Borough order on the board, does this: it looks at the Districts directly connected to it, inside its own Borough. That is its whole world. Among the mobs holding those districts, it hunts the one whose marker sits furthest right on the Heat Track; if none of them has a marker on the Track at all, it stays put. Then it picks the door: that mob's in-reach district with the most barrels (tie: the hottest boiler on the Borough's Pressure Strip), takes one step in, destroys any Safehouse, seizes every barrel, and scatters the crew. Then it squats there, an impassable wall, until the next Raid moves it on. When all four Squads have moved, every Heat marker on the Track goes home.

Three levers fall out of that paragraph, and they are the difference between a boss the law happens to and a boss who uses it.

Lever one: the law hunts the freshest name, not the worst one

The Squad doesn't count your markers. It reads the Track right to left and takes the first name it recognises: the most recent noise wins, however much older noise sits behind it. Three markers deep but quiet since Tuesday? The law walks past you to the crew that shouted this morning. One marker, placed last? You're the headline. So the discipline isn't "stay quiet"; nobody holding loud Jobs can afford that. It's be loud early in the cycle, then let your rivals shout over you. Every marker anyone else adds after yours is armour, and the boss who does his shooting the morning after a Raid, with the Track empty and four clean names behind him, has bought the longest quiet spell in the game: the whole table must out-shout him before the law comes knocking.

Lever two: reach is geography, and geography is yours to choose

A Squad's reach is one step, inside its own Borough. It cannot cross a Borough line, it cannot jump the water, and it cannot hunt what it cannot touch. Hold no district connected to a Squad and you are untouchable at any temperature: the hottest crew in the city, walking free, because the law can't reach the block you actually stand on. Read the board with that eye and the safe rooms light up: every district more than one step from a Squad, every district across a Borough line from one, and the whole of Staten Island, where no Squad is ever posted. The Staten lane from Part II isn't just uncontested by rivals; it is the one place in the game the law cannot follow you, which is worth remembering when your plan says "be the loudest crew in town." One caution: Squads resolve in Borough order, and a crew scattering from the first can land in the path of the second. When the Track sits at four, check your exits against every precinct, not just the nearest.

Lever three: plant the evidence, the decoy warehouse

You often can't stop the knock. You can almost always choose the door, because the Squad kicks in your most-barrelled district in its reach, and where your barrels sit is entirely up to you. So when your name is going to be freshest (and a boss running loud Jobs always knows when that is), you build the bust before the law does: pick the in-reach district you can best afford to lose, and make it the fattest target on the map. Pile the barrels you were about to sell anyway. Keep the Safehouse off it: the house is $500, a Play, and your only Recruit anchor, and the Raid burns it for free. Keep the Boss off it. Then let them come: the cops seize the plant, padlock a corner you'd already written off, and march straight past the empire. The fee is the barrels (so plant cheap Moonshine, not Rum) and the prize is everything else. One refinement for the connoisseur: the Squad has to step off something to step onto your plant. Early in the game that's a High Society door swinging open, and Part II already told you who's waiting outside it. One thing to mind on the way: the Squad padlocks whatever Still sits under the block it lands on, shutting that boiler cold until a later Raid throws it open, so build the plant on a corner whose brew you can spare.

Leave a back door, or don't leave at all. A raided crew flees to one Connected Safe District; and if there isn't one, because every exit is rival turf, water, the Blue Wall or the map's edge, the whole crew is arrested and boxed. That rule cuts twice. Defensively: never let a barrel pile and a dead-end share an address in Raid season. Offensively: the exits of a rival's likely bust are a thing you can quietly close. A Runner on this corner, a picket on that one, and the Raid you both saw coming stops being a fine and becomes a massacre. The law does the shooting. You just locked the doors.

When You Want the Fire

Everything above treats the Raid as a threat. Half the time it's the payoff. Count what the sirens actually deliver. Every Heat marker at the table goes home: the deepest refund flows to whoever banked the most noise, and a crew with three markers cooling gets three rungs of its ladder back in one breath. The doors open: each Squad that moves un-padlocks what it was sitting on, and the game starts with all four $500 rooms and all four 7-boilers under police boots; there is no High Society lane, no Opening Night, no wall of sevens, until somebody makes the city boil. And the board rearranges: safehouses burn, barrels vanish, garrisons scatter into each other's path. A Raid is the single most violent Play in the game, and nobody pays Influence for it.

So the real question about the fifth marker is never whether it lands. It's who chooses the moment, because the boss who places it fires every consequence above with the board arranged his way: his decoy fat, his exits open, his rivals' barrels massed for tomorrow's sales, their crews standing in reach. The catch is printed in Lever One: the trigger marker is by definition the freshest name on the Track, so the trigger man is the target, unless his turf is out of reach, or his decoy is already built, or the marker isn't his at all (see Part V for how to arrange that). At four markers, the whole Hustle changes character: every loud Play at the table is a finger on the trigger, and the boss with a clean position and a reason to want sirens should be racing to pull it first.

The Rat: A Raid on Demand

And then there's the clean trigger. The Rat Play fires an immediate Raid at any Track height, and (here is its teeth) it places no marker. The Track stays exactly as the last loud Play left it, which means the law hunts whoever fired the last loud shot, not you. A rival storms a district, kills three men, masses his barrels for the morning sale, and you drop a dime that same afternoon: his marker is freshest, his crew is in reach, his stockpile is the fattest door on the block. That is not a defensive rule. That is a precision air strike, purchasable for 2 Influence, and the only question is whether you can afford the price tag that comes with it.

Because the price is the Rat Card: no Bribing, no crown, no second call, until it leaves you; and it leaves only when your Boss dies or a rival Rats it off you. That makes the Rat a strictly early weapon: while your Influence ladder hasn't started, the Card costs you almost nothing, and The Insurance Job will even pay you 3 Respect for making the call, provided you've engineered the bust to land on a low-Pressure Still you Control, which is Lever Three run in reverse: make sure the last shout in town was yours, and stack the barrels on the boiler you want padlocked. Late game, the arithmetic flips entirely: a boss at 9 Influence who Rats has locked himself out of the throne and handed the table a hostage. And mind the hot potato: a rival who Rats later doesn't just trigger a Raid, he takes the Card off you, which is the one favour no one at the table means kindly.

Kingpin's Note

The greenhorn thinks Heat is a punishment and plays scared of his own gun. The old hand budgets it: he batches his noise, shouts early in the cycle, and keeps a borough line between his boilers and the blue wall. The made man aims it. He stacks his plant on a corner he never loved, walks the cops off the velvet room, collects his refund with the doors standing open; and when a rival makes the mistake of being loud and rich on the same afternoon, he finds a telephone. The law is a hammer lying in the middle of the table, and every fifth loud Play, somebody gets to pick it up. Make sure it's you.

Part V · The Table

Everything else in this book you can calculate. This chapter you have to play. The rules hand you three instruments (a loan, a handshake, and an off-book cash trade) and one warning printed in iron: there are no shared victories. Every deal in this game runs out at dusk, every partner is a rival on a schedule, and yet the tables in the other four chapters keep saying the same thing: the man who plays alone pays full price for everything. Here is how to buy cheaper.

Why Deal at All?

Because tempo is zero-sum, and a deal moves it. When two crews trade something each values less for something each values more (your spare Mash face for his walk-away at the Offers, your $1,000 for safe passage through Brooklyn), the tempo they save comes out of the two crews not at the table. Every fair deal is a conspiracy against the men not in it. That is the whole theory of cooperation in this game, and it's why the strongest tables are never quiet: a boss who won't deal isn't being careful, he's paying retail in a city that runs on wholesale.

And because the leader problem cannot be solved alone. Respect is fully public (completed Jobs, Deeds and Titles all sit face-up) and holdings top out at 14, so every crew must come through the Jobs Market to win. When one boss reaches 12, the counterplay is written in Part II: deny the crowning card. But below ten Influence a hate-draft costs the drafter two mornings and days of rent, which no single rival can afford to pay twice, so the containment has to be shared. A cartel of denial, each rival burning one Offer in rotation, splits a crippling bill into three affordable ones. The same logic runs everywhere: the table can leave the leader's number in the pool for the Blowback, the Harbormaster can name the face that starves him, and the man with the freshest marker on the Heat Track can be handed to the law by a single phone call. None of it needs a treaty. It needs three bosses reading the same board and each doing the cheap piece. Do it early: at ten Influence the leader's denial costs him nothing and yours still costs you everything.

What Actually Trades

  • Cash: The only thing that changes hands freely, off the board, any time. The grease in every deal below.
  • Liquor: By Move; barrels left in a rival's District become theirs. The rulebook calls it a donation; it is also the sanctioned way to pay in product, and a barrel of Rum is $300–$500 and a Kickback, the richest coin at the table.
  • The Mash: The Harbormaster names tomorrow's number for the whole city, and a face that fires an ally's boilers as well as his own costs him nothing. The cheapest favour in the game, and the easiest to collect on later.
  • The Market: A walk-away that frees a card, a claim that denies one, a morning's pick conceded. Priced in mornings, the scarcest currency there is.
  • The queue: Stepping off early or hanging on late to hand an ally the token he needs. Free to give whenever your own evening didn't matter.
  • Influence itself: By loan, never by gift. It deserves its own section, because the loan is the sharpest knife in the drawer.

Puppeteering: The Loan with a Fuse

At any time during the Hustle (not as a Play, not even on your turn) you may slide Influence from your Ledger to any boss still on the street. He may refuse. Accepted markers sit in his Ledger and he spends them as his own: you choose who gets the money, and that is the last thing you choose. Then the settlement rule, and read it like a contract, because it is one: a quiet Play returns your marker the moment it's spent; a loud one locks your marker on the Heat Track; and when two mobs' markers fund one Play, the boss making the Play picks whose marker takes the fall, even between two lenders. Four plays live inside that rule:

  • Heat laundering. The spender points the finger, so the spender's own markers can stay clean forever: ask for one marker "to cover the Unload," pay the Greed Tax with the lender's name, and walk away silent. Remember Part IV: the Track targets by freshness, and the marker on the Track belongs to its owner. A boss who goes loud on borrowed money has just made his banker the most wanted man in the city. Accepting a loan costs nothing. Giving one is signing a liability.
  • The poisoned gift. Run the same play from the other chair: lending to a crew with loud work left in its day is volunteering for the Track, so lend to quiet men, late in their day, when you can see what's left in front of them. And when a rival offers you markers unprompted, ask what he knows about tonight's sirens that you don't.
  • The proxy war. The loan works mid-firefight: a Pinned invader out of markers must Fall Back, and yours can keep him shooting. When two crews you don't like are locked in an alley, the cheapest Play on the board is a marker slipped to whichever one is losing. Whoever wins, the block comes out bleeding, and you never fired a shot. Price it honestly: if your marker funds the killing volley, expect the finger.
  • The mercy loan. Unspent loans pay the borrower Odd Jobs ($100 a marker when he steps off), then come home to your Reserves. Lend three markers to a broke ally at the end of his day and you've handed him $300 without opening your wallet; it cost you the same $300 your own Lay Low would have paid. A legal bribe, denominated in your idle afternoon, and it keeps a man on the street who'd otherwise have stepped off, which on the right evening is the whole staring contest bought for a smile.

The uncommitted Ledger is a diplomatic instrument. Because the loan works at any moment, three unspent markers in front of you change every negotiation at the table before a single one moves: the invader deciding whether to fire again doesn't know whose side your markers are on; neither does the man he's firing at. Sit on your tempo late in the day and you are, briefly, the bank; and the bank sets terms.

The Handshake: Collateral and Sabotage

When a deal needs teeth, the promiser stakes one Influence marker where the table can see it. Terms must be clear, and every deal dies at the end of the current Day. Kept: the marker goes home. Broken, for any reason, including somebody else's interference: the marker is forfeit to the box. Not the Track, not the Reserves. Gone, a permanent rung off the ladder, $2,500 and a Play to buy back. Three consequences follow, and only the first one is friendly:

  • Promise only what you alone control. "I will not cross the river today" cannot be sabotaged: the only person who can break it is you. "You'll cross my Borough unharmed" is a promise about the whole table's behaviour, and any third crew can make a liar of you with one Move. Word your deals as your own actions, never as outcomes, and read every deal you're offered the same way: who besides him can break this?
  • A rival's handshake is a target. The forfeit falls on the promiser however the terms fail, so the moment two rivals stake a deal, sabotaging it joins your list of available Plays. Block the passage, shoot the man he promised to protect, rob the courier carrying the payment. One Move of yours, and a rival pays the box a marker he meant to wear as a crown.
  • Collateral appreciates. The staked marker still counts toward your ten, so mid-game a handshake is nearly free to offer: honest reputation, bought on margin. But at the endgame the same marker is mortal: a boss at 10 Influence who breaks a deal (or has one broken for him) un-crowns himself on the spot. If you must promise on the night you mean to win, promise small, promise early, and promise nothing a rival can touch.

The Off-Book Sale: Arbitrage in Barrels

The third instrument the rules hand you is the plainest: cash changes hands off the board, any time, for anything. Most bosses spend it on a walk-away or safe passage. The sharper trade is product. A barrel is worth a flat $300 in your Speakeasy and a fat $500 under a High Society chandelier, and the gap between those two numbers is a business, if you can find a man who owns the better address.

The High Society arbitrage

Say a rival holds one of the four $500 rooms and you don't. You have barrels and no premium joint to pour them in; he has the joint and, this evening, better things to do than fill it. So you Move the barrels into his District (Cost 1), and by the rulebook's own donation rule they become his the moment they land. He hands you $300 in cash and Unloads them for $500 under the lights. Read that ledger and it is the rarest thing at this table, an honest win for both chairs: you banked your $300 and spent a Move where you'd have spent an Unload, one marker cheaper; he pocketed the $200 premium your own address could never have paid you. The High Society tax the Police collect at setup, the two of you quietly split between yourselves.

The standing shop

Turn that one sale into a trade and post a price. “I'll pay $300 for any barrel delivered to a room I control today.” Back the words with a Handshake so the table knows the money is real, and let the whole city run your distribution: every rival with spare Moonshine and no premium room now has somewhere to dump it, and every barrel that arrives you sell on at $500. One caution, and it is the whole of Part IV in a sentence: a pile of barrels is a target. The Raid kicks the fattest-barrelled door in reach, so a shop that fills up on the wrong afternoon is a decoy warehouse you built for the cops, by accident. Run it, but keep your Safehouse and your Boss on another block, and know the mornings your own name sits freshest on the Track.

The Rum premium

One product trades above the rate. A barrel of Havana Rum pays whoever Unloads it the same cash plus a Kickback, that late-day marker from Part II that hands a boss his afternoon back. So Rum is never a $300 barrel in a deal; it is $300 and a Play, and you charge for both. Move Rum to a partner's room and the premium is yours to name: he still comes out ahead, because the Kickback he buys off you is worth more to a boss running dry at dusk than the coins it cost him. The richest coin at the table, changing hands at a price the coin itself sets.

Conspiracies: Aiming the Table at One Man

Every deal above is two crews splitting a profit. The deadlier ones split a victim. When the board hands you and a rival the same enemy, the cheapest Play on the board is often the one you make on his behalf.

Mutual targets: back his war for your prize

Two rivals lock horns over a Borough you don't want. Read past the Borough. If the man on the defensive holds a Dock, and losing it would tip the Harbormaster's crown onto your head (most Docks, Part I), then his war is your business. Slide the attacker a marker mid-firefight, the proxy war from the loan section, and help him win a battle whose spoils you never touch: he takes the turf, you take back the dice. The trick is to want a different prize than the men doing the bleeding. They fight for the ground; you fight for what the ground controls.

Catching the leader: the cartel of denial, in moves

Part II says the crown is denied at the Jobs Market, and no single rival can afford that denial twice. Here is what the cartel actually does, once three bosses agree the leader has run far enough:

  • Leave his number in the pool. Conspire to forgo a Red you'd each have liked, so the leader's firing face sits in the pool as the Blowback and bursts the boiler he parked his Boss on. Nobody fired a shot; the dice did it, because three men agreed not to draft.
  • Fund the man who picks last. Fake generosity is the sharpest kind. Lend markers to a rival late in his day so he outlasts the field and Lays Low last, claiming the highest Turn Token. By the Reverse Snake (Part II) the last man off the street picks the Market first, ahead of the leader, and lifts the crowning card out from under him: on your dime, under the rival's name.
  • Pay a rival to strike the match. A cash bounty rents another crew's signature Play against the leader: hire the Knights to Torch his Safehouse (his only Recruit anchor), or the Sicilians to put a Hit on his Boss. Off-book cash is free to promise, and the man you pay wears the Heat, not you.
  • Corner him, then call. Pay a rival to plant a Runner across the leader's one open exit, then play the Rat. A raided crew with no Connected Safe District is not fined, it is boxed (Part IV). You bought the lock; the law kicks the door.
  • Sell his barrels back to him. If the leader is barrel-rich and Speakeasy-poor, take his Moonshine into your High Society, Unload it at $500, and split the premium. He turns product into Respect's fuel; you skim the difference and read the shape of his plan off what he asks you to sell.

Put out a hit. The purest conspiracy needs no partner, only a wallet. When you and a rival are racing for the same Offer, don't out-run him, un-man him: post a bounty the whole table can collect. “$1,000 to whoever kills the Irish Boss today.” A crew with no Boss on the board skips The Offers the next morning (Part II), so the card you both wanted falls to you uncontested, and the killing was somebody else's gun, somebody else's Heat, and your money. The bounty is free to name; you only pay if it works.

The Frame-Up: Selling the Raid

Part IV taught you to aim the law at your own decoy. The same aim works on a rival, and a deal is how you take it. The Raid kicks the fattest-barrelled door in each Squad's reach, so the man with the biggest stockpile chooses nothing and loses everything, and where the barrels sit is a thing you can arrange.

  • Frame a rival. Before you drop a dime, donate barrels onto a rival's block, fattening his District into the obvious target. Then Rat, or simply wait for the fifth marker to land: the cops walk past your empire and kick the door you stocked for them.
  • The double cross. Move him liquor to sell, shake on it, and drop the dime the same afternoon. He masses the barrels for tomorrow's sale exactly as you asked; the Raid reads that pile as a confession, and you watch the cops break down a door you loaded. The Handshake even leaves you looking like a partner, right up to the sirens.
  • The liquor defense. Run it backwards to guard your own turf. When a rival covets a District of yours and the Track runs hot, leave barrels on it. Take the block by force and he draws Heat in the taking (the freshest marker on the Track) and inherits the stockpile he just captured. One phone call later he is the loudest, richest man in reach: caught red-handed, holding your barrels, his own name freshest on the sheet. The turf you “lost” was the bait.

Specialist Services: Renting the Signature Play

Every family owns two Plays no rival can copy, and a monopoly is a shop. What your crew can do and theirs cannot, they will pay to rent, because the alternative is not for sale at any price.

  • The Sicilians sell cool. Only Consigliere lifts a marker off the Heat Track and hands it back to its owner's Reserves. To a rival cooking loud and dreading the sirens, that one phone call is worth real money, and the Syndicate is the only number that answers it.
  • The Knights sell fire and water. Their Torch is the cheapest way in the city to un-house a rival's Safehouse, and their Skiff runs five barrels clean across the water in a single Play. Sell the Torch as a weapon aimed at a third crew, and the Skiff as a freight line, the Staten lane on demand. The Network was always a business; the customers are just other bosses now.

Price every one of these the way the loan sharks price a debt. You are not being generous; you are the sole wholesaler of a thing your buyer cannot make, and the seams of this book, the moments where one chapter pays for another, are exactly where that thing is dearest.

The Blood Oath: Politics with a Deadline

The team variant turns everything above up to a boil, because the Federal Crackdown Tracker puts a date on the politics: every Raid advances it, and at space 10 the table freezes, ranks by Respect, and marries 1st to 4th, 2nd to 3rd, permanently. Play toward that moment, not just through it:

  • Your rank at the Sit-Down is a position, and you can play it. First place buys the weakest partner in the game; second place buys the balanced marriage. With the token on 8 or 9, a claim-late boss can sandbag a completed-Job-in-waiting for a morning (or sprint one home) to choose which wedding he attends. The Targets scale (combined Respect +10), so the race resets either way; what doesn't reset is whose engine you're chained to, and whose home borough borders yours.
  • The clock itself is playable. Raids advance it, and Part IV taught you to fire Raids on demand. A bottom-of-the-table boss who Rats is not just calling the law: he's dragging the wedding forward while he's still cheap enough to marry the leader. A leader enjoying the solo game keeps the city cool for exactly the same reason.
  • Watch the honor you're marrying. No Rat and no debtor can be crowned, and in the Blood Oath, neither can their Alliance. That fourth-place crew with the Rat Card and two of Shylock's Marks isn't a bargain partner; he's a padlock on your throne until he's clean. The Sit-Down doesn't launder anybody.
  • After the oath, the loan comes off the leash. Puppeteering your partner is the engine of the whole variant: the rich crew funds the one with the cheap Respect in front of it, Kickbacks and all. But the finger rule still applies inside the family (your partner's loud Play on your marker is your name on the Track), and so does the fine print of turf: his District is still Hostile ground, and his barrels are still his. Blood is thicker than water. It is not thicker than the rules.
  • And the family has one throne. The Capo di Tutti Capi is whoever contributed the most Respect to the winning total. The variant's last joke is that the race never ends: help your partner exactly enough to win, and not one Job more.
Kingpin's Note

In this business a favour is a loan, a loan is a leash, and a handshake is a knife you hand a stranger handle-first. The greenhorn keeps his word out of decency and wonders why it keeps costing him. The old hand keeps it because the marker's watching. The made man writes terms only he can break, lends only to quiet men, points the finger like he's passing the salt; and when two rivals shake hands across the table, he starts pricing what it would cost to make one of them a liar. There are no shared victories in this city. There are only bills, and better company to split them with.

The Whole Day at Once

Five chapters, one machine. Watch a single Day through all five lenses and the seams show: at dawn you choose a number, and the docks choose whether you meant it. In the morning you reach for a card, or pointedly don't, and four people read the difference. All day you spend tempo (on turf, on volleys, on one more hour in the staring contest) while the law bills the noise to the only account you've got. And at nightfall the queue you fell into prices tomorrow: dice for the committed, cards for the free.

Three Questions Before Every Play

Which clock does this advance? The cheap Respect draining out of the deck, the ladder from six to ten, or the slow boil of the Track. A Play that advances none of them is a Play a rival would pay you to make. What does it tell the table? Your Jobs, your Respect, your stake, your barrels and your Heat are all public; your intention is the only secret you own, and most Plays spend a little of it. And who hears it? Every noise has a bill, and the bill has a name on it: make sure it's the right name, even if it isn't yours.
