THE ALMANAC

Odds, Angles and Advice from the Last Night at Lindy's.

Lindy's, on Broadway, the night of 4 November 1928. The man in the back booth does business here until dawn. They call him The Brain: Arnold Rothstein bankrolled some of the first rum boats, is said to have fixed a World Series, and gave half the young guns in town their start. Tonight he's talking to one of them, a hard kid called Charlie Lucania. In two days Rothstein will be dead, and New York will go to war over what he leaves behind.

Sit Down, Charlie

Sit down, Charlie. Have the cheesecake; it's the only honest thing on Broadway. You've learned the rules, which puts you level with every sucker in this city. The rules tell you what you're allowed to do; I'm going to tell you what pays. I've made my living on the difference.

1.Count your Plays before you count your money.

Plays today  =  Influence  −  staked  −  Heat,  up to five.

That's the whole racket in one line. Every marker you own is sitting on a Job, cooling on the Heat Track, or working in your Ledger, and only the working ones buy you anything today. The Ledger stops at five, so the markers above your fifth don't buy extra Plays. What they buy is room: room to stake a Job, room to make some noise, and still wake up with five in the drawer. Half of what I tell you tonight is a fight over one of those three terms.

2.Your token's a trade, not a rank.

Suckers think Turn Token #1 is the best seat. It's a seat. The low token drafts first at the Brew and moves first in the Hustle. The high token picks first at the Offers, and at the Brew it picks from the last two dice, so it decides which one is left to blow. Dice or cards: that's the trade.

You don't choose your token; your Ledger does, because tomorrow's tokens go out in the order men Lay Low. The man carrying a fat stake runs dry early and wakes up with the dice. The man carrying nothing is still on the street at dusk and wakes up with first pick of the Market. So plan tonight from the morning you want. When tomorrow's Mash is your number, go home early. When there's a card on the street you can finish tomorrow, stay out late, and if you've nothing worth doing, Collect. It pays what the marker would have paid you anyway, and it holds your seat without showing anybody a thing.

I · The Stills

Every sucker in this city thinks the dice decide who eats. I've spent my whole life around dice, Charlie. They decide a lot less than people think.

3.Cook sevens.

If you remember one thing I tell you tonight, make it this. The Harbormaster names tomorrow's Mash, and a mean one can leave your boilers stone cold: let him name a 1, and no Red die in the world makes an 8. But a 7? Whatever face he names, 1 through 6, some Red makes seven. Nobody can starve a 7.

And sevens are the only number worth stacking. Count the boilers: four 7s in the city, three 4s, three 6s, a pair of everything else, and one lonely 12 up in East Harlem. A wall of 8s is two kettles and a light switch. The catch is that every 7 sits under a High Society room, and the cops start the game on all four. Each opens when a Raid moves its Squad along, so be standing next door when yours does: the boiler and the Kickback come through the same door. Crew each Still with five men and stop there. The sixth brews nothing the fifth didn't.

4.Can't get sevens? Bracket the ends.

Hold one low boiler and one high one, like a 3 and a 9. The face that starves the 3 is the face that feeds the 9, and the other way round. He can squeeze one or the other, never both. You'll never pour like a fat stack, but you'll pour something every morning, and when a hostile Harbormaster is starving the table, something is a fortune.

The best bracket in town is Whitestone's 5 and Throggs Neck's 10. A bridge joins them, so a Boss on one can Split the Batch into the other, and both are Docks, so they count toward the Harbormaster besides.

5.Read the Harbormaster, then beat him to bed.

The man who names the Mash cooks from the same pot you do. If his Stills are 8s and 9s, he'll name a middling face or a high one, because a low one leaves his own boilers cold. His turf tells you tomorrow's number a full day early. So build on his numbers: whatever face lights his boilers lights yours, and he can only starve you by starving himself.

When he clumps on one number, the Mash he names leaves exactly one face on the dice that fires it. So mirror his Stills and Lay Low before he does, and you draft ahead of him tomorrow. Pick first and you fire whenever that face shows anywhere in the pool, about three mornings in five at a table of four. Pick behind him and it has to show twice: one morning in five, at best. He built the shortage; you walk off with the only key. The early night costs you Plays and a late seat at the Offers. Against a man clumped on one number, pay it.

6.Take the piers, or tie them.

Better than reading the Harbormaster is being him, and nobody elects him. He's whoever holds the most Docks, and every Dock in the city is one Move from every other. Everybody starts with one, and at least four more stand empty: the Bowery, Whitestone, and Staten Island's two. Put one Runner on a second pier and you name the number every morning until somebody matches you. Cook sevens as well and you can name whatever face hurts the table most, because nothing you name can starve a 7.

You don't have to lead to spoil it, either. When the lead is tied, nobody names the Mash and the die rolls. So when a hostile Harbormaster is squeezing you, don't fight him for the piers. Tie him, with one man on one empty Dock, and let the dice have it back.

7.Park the Boss beside a cold boiler.

Your Boss is worth a second Still every morning his own one fires: Split the Batch lets you fire one more Still you Control next door, whatever its number. So stand him on a boiler that fires often, beside a fat one that doesn't. Sugar Hill's 7 sits across the avenue from East Harlem's 12, the loneliest boiler in town. Hold both, and that 12 fires every time the 7 does.

Just remember his number is his payday and his danger. The Blowback comes off the same dice, and every man it takes off that block is one fewer standing between the old man and a raid (lesson 22). He walks one block a Play like anybody else, so move him the night before, not the morning of.

8.Your seat only matters when your number shows twice.

Amateurs sweat the Blowback every morning. Don't. The Red you take leaves the pool with you, so if the face you need showed up once, nothing left on the table can burn that boiler. The danger is a double: two of your face, you take one, and some sweetheart leaves the other lying there as the Blowback. That's the morning to worry, and the morning the last seat matters, because he picks from the final two dice and decides which one stays.

What I've Seen

The greenhorn spreads his Stills across four Boroughs to cover more numbers, lights one boiler a day and feeds the rest to the Blowback. The old hand cooks one number and gets rich. The made man cooks down one street, where his Boss can walk a batch from door to door, and keeps a man on a second pier, so the number is his to name.

II · The Offers

The Jobs Market is where Respect is for sale. Suckers treat it like a shop. It's a queue, and you pay rent to stand in it.

9.The stake is rent. Claim the morning you fire.

At 6 Influence, a 5-Respect Job costs you two Plays a day for as long as you hold it. Claim it three days early and you've paid six Plays for a promise. But nothing on the street expires, and every Job in the deck is done in a single Play. So build the position first, quietly, then claim the card on the morning you can do the deed that afternoon. One day of rent, full Respect.

The trouble is that your board talks even when your cards don't: barrels drifting toward one bar, a Safehouse creeping out of its Borough, men gathering two blocks from a Dock. A rival who reads turf sees the claim coming and takes the card first. So assemble late and fast. Six barrels arriving in one Move on the morning you claim is a done deal; six arriving over four days is an announcement. When the build can't be hidden, pay the rent and claim early.

And a claim can lie for you. Take a card that points at Manhattan and watch the Manhattan boss thicken his garrison while your real business happens somewhere else. You pay rent on the feint, but you're buying three men looking the wrong way.

10.Bribe early; it's a Jobs play.

Most men think Bribes are for the end, when you're buying your way to the table. Take it from a man who's bought a few judges: wrong. Your 7th marker makes a 3-Respect Job free to hold, and your 8th makes a 5 free. They're the cheapest rungs on the ladder, $2,000 and $3,000, and they do the most work. The 9th and 10th are headroom for the noise you'll make, and the 10th gets you the Nod besides: ten Respect, half your seat at the Commission, in one envelope. Keep the old man on the board while you climb; he makes every drop in person.

The money's in the footprint. Extort pays $200 for every block you hold, anywhere in town, and a Runner costs $300. So a man standing alone on an empty corner has paid for himself the second time you shake the city down, and after that he's buying judges. Eight blocks is $1,600 a shakedown.

11.Eat the 1s while they last.

There are twelve 1-Respect Jobs in the deck, and half of them ask for what your liquor business does anyway: haul some barrels, sell some barrels, make a Trade. A Stake of 1 costs nothing at 6 Influence. Every one that gets done leaves the deck for good, and the cheap ones go first. The man who sniffs at 1s on Day 3 is shopping in a market full of wars on Day 12.

12.Every seat has a free 3 in its back yard.

Each home Borough has one Speakeasy that nobody garrisons at setup and the cops don't padlock, and the deck has a 3-Respect Job written for every one of them. Take yours early, before somebody garrisons it to spite you.

13.Staten Island: nine Respect and no cops.

Staten Island is nobody's home turf, so nobody goes there. But the deck has a Job out there at every tier: Fortress Staten (1), Rum Row (3) and The Smuggler's Run (5). That's nine Respect nobody's racing you for. Every Dock in the city reaches Staten's two piers, both count toward the Harbormaster, and Stapleton is a Ward nobody starts in (lessons 6 and 23). No Police Squad ever sets foot on the island. The price is distance: it's a long way from home, and nobody will help you hold it.

14.Only deny a card that would crown somebody.

You can take any card on the street, including one you never mean to do, just to keep it out of a rival's hands. It works, and it's dear: the morning you take it, a second morning to walk away from it, and rent every day in between. Save it for the card that would put a rival over 20. Then it's cheap at any price, because there's no tomorrow left to save for.

15.Pour Rum at High Society late, and in batches.

Here's the one way to get more than five Plays in a day. Every barrel of Havana Rum you Unload at a High Society Venue you Control moves a marker from your Reserves back into an empty Ledger slot. Three barrels pay for the Unload and buy you a Play, and three is too few for the Greed Tax to hear. Five is the ceiling.

There are two ways to waste it. Pour early, with your Ledger full, and there's no empty slot for the Kickback to land in, so pour on your last markers. And don't make the run every day: Moonshine to the Dock, Trade, Rum to the room costs Plays before it pays any back. A daily run of four barrels loses you Plays. Stockpile ten, make the trip once, pour it in two Unloads, and you net about three, for two Heat markers. Just remember a stockpile is exactly what the cops come looking for.

What I've Seen

The greenhorn grabs the biggest card on the street the day he sees it, pays two Plays a morning for a week, tells the whole table what he's building, and watches the target walk out of the Borough. The old hand takes the small ones his liquor was doing anyway. The made man climbs to ten Influence, where a promise costs him nothing, claims on the morning he fires, and takes the last card off the street on the night the second-place man needed it.

III · The Gun

Every fight is a bet, Charlie, and most men place it without looking at the odds. Guns don't win this town; Respect does. But turf never comes quietly, so learn which fights pay.

16.Never walk into even numbers, and never grind.

Defense is strong in this city. Take a block held by a Boss, three Runners and a Safehouse. Bring your own Boss and three men, even numbers, and you take it about one try in seventy. Bring double and it's a coin flip. Bring triple and it's yours nearly nine times in ten, and you'll still bury four or five men getting in the door.

Every volley costs a marker, too. Plan to win in two or three. If the door isn't breaking, Fall Back. A fourth volley buys you about eight points of odds and costs a Play you could have spent anywhere else.

17.The fight you'll win is the Boss and a few men against a picket.

The fortress is the exception: nobody has the men to build more than one. Every other block your rival holds is watched by one man, two, three, or nobody, and that's where fights are won. Your Boss and three Runners into a two-man picket takes it 84% of the time, for about one body. Three men with the Boss beat five without him, because his Threat sharpens every die you roll. Don't leave the old man at home when it counts.

18.Hit him when his Ledger's empty.

A man with no marker in his Ledger can't Ambush, and neither can a man who's Laid Low. Catch a picket then and you take it more often, and you bury a third to a half fewer men doing it. The price is that you fire first, so the Heat is yours. Watch the Ledgers around the table: when a man's spent out behind a block worth owning, that's your afternoon.

19.Bide your time.

Don't wait for that afternoon, Charlie; arrange it. Nobody passes in the Hustle, so every round costs every man a marker. Collect costs you exactly one and moves nothing, while every Power Play he makes and every Ambush he fires puts him further behind you. So keep your Ledger fat, count his, and let him spend himself dry before you knock. Sit behind him in the Turn Order and you need one Collect fewer: he makes his last Play before you make yours.

Just don't overdo it, because every Collect is a volley you won't fire. Take five markers, a Boss and four, and the house every family starts in. Strike at once and you take it one time in three and bury four men; one Collect later, into a man who can't Ambush, it's three times in four for two. A second Collect still pays against a Boss behind his Safehouse, and nowhere else. A third never does. And he can see you waiting: his last marker can Recruit men into his house or walk the barrels out. So stand where you threaten two blocks, and count his Rum as markers.

20.Two men is a picket; five is a wall.

Now sit on the other side of the table. The Muscle Ratio gives a block one die for every two men, rounded up, so the third and the fifth man each bring a new gun, and the fourth is the one who lets you lose a man and keep shooting. Against the raid you'll see most, a Boss and four, that makes two men a gift, three a gamble and five a wall: he gets through five about one time in sixteen. Past five it hardly matters; a sixth man buys you about five points.

That's why five is the number everywhere in this town. Five men on a Still brew three barrels, and the same five hold the block against anything short of an army. Bring a Boss and eight and he's through two times in three. So decide what every block is before anybody walks in. The blocks your win lives on get five. Everything else gets one man, to hold the Extort and the boiler, and he Folds when company comes.

21.Fold, check or raise.

When a man walks onto your block you've got three answers, same as poker, and I know poker. Fold if the corner's scrap: you keep every man, and men are harder to replace than turf. Hold Fire is the check. He has to shoot first to find out what you've got, he pays for every volley, and he takes the Heat for opening, while you shoot back for free. Ambush is the raise, and raises are for made hands. With two dice or more it swings a fight. With one die it kills half a man on average and puts your marker on the Track. Weak hands check or fold. Strong hands raise.

And read his Jobs before you answer. A Fold is the one answer that leaves no bodies, and some cards pay only in bodies: The Toll Booth Trap wants a Boss dead in Queens, The Butcher's Ledger five men dead in Brooklyn. Fold to a man holding one and he's paid rent for an empty room. The smart ones know it, so they close your exits before they knock: with no Safe block to run to, you can't Fold.

22.Go for the head when he walks thin.

Runners take the hits before the Boss does, in every fight but a Sicilian Hit. So a Boss behind four men and his Safehouse is safe from everybody but the Sicilians, and a Boss with one man beside him is two bullets from the supply. The old man has errands, though. He Splits the Batch from a boiler, he sharpens every fight he walks into, and the Blowback thins the men around him. Watch where he goes and who goes with him. Against a thin Boss the plain gun does the job; the Hit is for the man behind a crowd.

And don't trust the house he started in. A Boss, two men and a Safehouse is how every family begins; catch it spent out, and a Boss and four take the old man three times in four. What you buy is more than a body. Until he Rises, his family fights without its Threat, brews without its Split, can't Bribe and can't be crowned.

23.A dead man needs a Ward.

Here's the follow-through most men forget. A dead Boss comes back one way: Rise, two markers, and only into a Safe Ward. There are five Wards in New York, one to a Borough. Every family starts in its own, and the others' Wards aren't Safe for him while their owners hold them. That leaves him his own, Stapleton out on Staten Island, and at a table of three, the Ward in the Borough nobody took.

So when you kill a man's Boss, take the Ward he'd come home to, and his house with it if it's standing there (lesson 24). Then put one Runner on every Ward that's left open. Now he has nowhere to Rise, and every day he spends fighting his way back into a Ward, he fights without the old man's Threat, and if you left five in his, into a wall. A man at 19 Respect with no Boss and no Ward is going nowhere, whatever his pile says.

24.Armies that don't march.

Fifteen Runners can't be everywhere, but your Safehouse can go anywhere Safe, and it's the only place you Recruit. So don't march an army to the border. Secure the Safehouse one step from the target, $500 and a Play, then Recruit right there at $300 a man. The garrison next door wakes up to five strangers who never crossed a street. Do it late in the day: between the Secure and the Recruit that house stands empty, and any rival next door with a marker left can walk in and take it.

Cheaper still, take his. Win a block with a rival's Safehouse in it and you can move yours in on the spot: no $500, no extra Play, and your men already standing in it. He loses the only place he can Recruit, you gain a front door in his Borough, and The Eviction pays three Respect for it.

25.Know your family's edge.

The Irish bring an extra die to every Open Fire, worth about a whole tier of men: a Boss and four Runners storming a Boss, three Runners and a Safehouse goes from about 8% to about 33%. It never fires on defense, so the Irish win by going through doors. Peddle sells swill in your own Wards for half the Influence an Unload costs.

The Sicilians don't take the turf; they take the man. A Hit kills a Boss behind a full garrison three times in five, where a plain gun almost never does, and lesson 23 keeps him dead. Untouchable keeps the cops off your Safehouse block, and Consigliere takes a marker off the Heat Track. Anybody's marker, which re-aims the next Raid.

The Vipers take away the Ambush and the Fold. Against a thin block that's everything: five men on a Stealth take a Boss, two Runners and a Safehouse three times in four, where the front door takes it one time in four. Never lay siege with it. Tunnel empties the till before the fight, and Whispers lets you hold a Job nobody can read.

The Knights don't add dice; they add reach. Skiff lands five men on any coast from any coast you Control, and after a Fold or an Advance it takes them off again at no extra cost. Anybody who misses the boat is lost, and no boat outruns the cops. Network puts a free Rum on every Trade, and Torch burns a Safehouse outright for 1 Influence and a Runner.

What I've Seen

The hothead charges a fortress with even numbers and buries four men. The corner boy learns that lesson too well, swears off the gun, and watches his turf walk away a picket at a time. The made man puts five men where his win lives and one everywhere else, and spends his afternoons counting Ledgers. When a rival's Boss walks out with one man beside him, he doesn't take the block. He takes the man, and has a Runner on Stapleton before the chair can be filled.

IV · The Law

Most men treat the cops like weather. I've paid enough of them to know better: the law is a machine, with a trigger, a way of picking a door, and a refund. Learn to read it and you can aim it.

26.The law counts Plays, not corpses.

Only four things put a marker on the Heat Track: the first shot of a firefight, Extort, Torch, and selling four or more barrels in one Play. One firefight is one marker however many men fall. Extort is one marker whether three blocks pay or eleven. Three barrels at a time is silent forever. So when you have to be loud, be loud in bulk. And the quiet answers cost nothing: Hold Fire and Fold never draw Heat.

27.The law chases the freshest name.

When the sirens come, each Squad goes after the mob in its reach whose marker sits furthest right on the Track: the most recent noise, not the most noise. Three markers deep but quiet since Tuesday? They'll walk right past you to the fellow who shouted this morning. So be loud early in the cycle, right after a Raid, and let the others shout over you. Every marker that lands after yours is armor.

28.A cop can only reach one block, and never across a Borough line.

A Squad only looks at the Districts right beside it, inside its own Borough. That's its whole world. Hold nothing next to a Squad and you can be the loudest crew in town and never see a badge. Staten Island has no Squad at all. One warning: Squads move one at a time in Borough order, so a crew scattering from the first can land in the path of the second. When the Track sits at four, check your exits against every precinct.

And learn your own precinct on the first morning. The Queens Squad stands beside Corona and the Brooklyn Squad beside Brownsville, so those two families start with their Safehouse inside a cop's reach. A Squad only hunts the names it can reach, so while you're alone in its precinct, one marker of yours on the Track when the sirens go is enough. It kicks in the house, unless you're Sicilian or you've given it a fatter door (lesson 29). Manhattan's Squad reaches your starting bar and pier; the Bronx's, your pier.

29.Build the bust yourself.

You can't always stop the knock, but you can pick the door. The Squad kicks in whichever of your blocks in its reach holds the most barrels. So when you know your name will be freshest, make the fattest target a corner you can afford to lose. Pile cheap Moonshine there, keep your Safehouse and your Boss somewhere else, and let them come. They take the swill, padlock a corner you'd written off, and walk straight past the empire.

Now turn it around. A raided crew runs on foot to a Safe block next door, and if there isn't one, the whole crew is arrested. A Dock with one land exit is a cell with a view. Put a man across a rival's only way out before the sirens, and the Raid you both saw coming stops being a fine and becomes a clean sweep.

30.At four Heat, let him fire the fifth.

When the Track sits at four, every loud Play is a finger on the trigger. So if a man walks onto your block, Hold Fire. If he wants it, he has to shoot first, and his first shot is the fifth marker with his name on it. Heat lands when his Play ends, so he gets to finish the fight, and then the sirens come for the freshest name on the Track: his.

Now leave a stack of barrels on that block, inside a Squad's reach. He knows before he pulls the trigger that whatever he wins, he'll be standing on the fattest door in the precinct when the cops arrive. That's a garrison that never fires a shot. The other side of the coin: at four, never fire first into a quiet block yourself. The man who checked to you may be holding the door open between you and the sirens.

31.Sometimes you want the sirens.

A Raid isn't only trouble. When it's over, every Heat marker at the table goes home, and whoever had the most cooling gets the most back. Every Squad that moves unlocks the block it was standing on, and early in the game that's a High Society room and its 7. So at four markers, the man with a clean position and a reason to want sirens should be racing to pull the trigger.

Can't wait? Drop a dime. The Rat starts a Raid right now, at any height, and puts no marker on the Track, so the law hunts whoever made the last noise, not you. You take the Rat Card: −2 Respect, but only when the books open. Far from 20, that's nearly free, and The Insurance Job pays 3 Respect for a Rat that padlocks one of your own weak Stills. One Sunset from the crown, it's most of a Job.

What I've Seen

The greenhorn thinks Heat is a punishment and plays scared of his own gun. The old hand budgets it: he batches his noise, shouts early in the cycle, and keeps a Borough line between his boilers and the blue wall. The made man aims it. He builds his bust on a corner he never loved, collects his refund with the doors standing open, and when a rival is loud and rich on the same afternoon, he finds a telephone.

V · The Table

Everything else I told you, you can count. This part you have to play. There are no shared victories in this city, Charlie. There are only bills, and better company to split them with.

32.Promise only what you alone control.

A broken Handshake costs you a point of Respect forever, and it counts however it breaks, even if somebody else broke it for you. So word every deal as something you do, never as something that happens. "I won't cross the river today" can't be sabotaged. "You'll cross my Borough unharmed" can be ruined by any third crew with one Move, and you're the one wearing the Welsher card.

Now turn it round. The moment two rivals shake on a promise like that, breaking it is one of your Plays. Block the road he swore was clear, shoot the man he swore to protect, and a rival is a point short for good.

33.Count a man's distance from 20 before you count on his word.

Respect is public, so the price of a promise is on the table for anyone to read. A man one point from the crown will die before he breaks his word. A man a long way back, or already wearing the Rat's −2, is selling paper the street has already discounted. And count the Welsher cards in his pile: a serial breaker's word isn't collateral, it's confetti.

34.Read the ladder, not the pile.

Everybody counts the piles, and most of them count wrong. The Nod is ten Respect in a single Play, bought the moment a man can pay for his 10th marker. So a man with ten Respect in Jobs and nine Influence isn't halfway to the crown. He's one envelope from it: $5,000 and a Play, any afternoon he likes. The man beside him with fourteen in Jobs and seven Influence looks closer and has the longer road: $12,000 of judges, or six more Respect off a Market that's run out of cheap work.

So add the ladder to the pile. Count his markers, price his next rung, and watch how much he's been selling. And remember that the old man makes every drop in person. Kill the Boss of a man sitting on nine, and his envelope stays in his pocket.

35.When a man nears 20, the table works together.

Nobody stops a runaway alone: bullets and denial cost too much for one crew to pay twice. So split the bill, each of you doing the cheap piece.

  • Leave his number in the pool. When the face that fires his Boss's boiler shows up twice, he can only take one. If nobody takes the other, it's the Blowback, and it strips the men standing round the old man.
  • Rent the last seat. The highest token picks the Market first. Pay that man to take the card the leader needs.
  • Put out a hit. No boss takes the city with his Boss in the supply. Post a cash bounty on the leader's Boss, and post it late in the day, when he's Laid Low or too spent to Rise before the books open. Then sit on the Wards (lesson 23), and one dead Boss buys the table more than one night.

36.In the Blood Oath, the wedding is a move.

In the team game every Raid moves the Federal Crackdown Tracker, and at space 10 the table freezes, ranks by Respect, and marries 1st to 4th and 2nd to 3rd, for good. Play toward that moment. Your rank picks your partner, and you can move it: a man at the bottom who Rats drags the wedding forward while he's still cheap enough to marry the leader. And look hard at the debts you're marrying. No Alliance is crowned while either partner holds a Mark, so his Mark is your padlock until he's clean. At $2,000 a Mark, and with cash free to cross the table, that's a bill you can pay for him.

Before You Go

Three questions, before every Play. Which clock does it move? The cheap Respect draining out of the deck, the climb from six Influence to ten, or the slow boil of the Heat Track. What does it tell the table? Your Jobs, your stake, your barrels and your Heat are all out in the open; what you mean to do next is the only secret you own, and most Plays spend a little of it. Who hears it? Every noise has a bill with a name on it. Make sure it's the right name, even when it isn't yours.

New York doesn't crown the man with the best dice, the best cards or the best guns, Charlie. It crowns the one who works out first that they were all the same money.

The phone rings at the counter. He listens, hangs up, and reaches for his coat. "McManus wants to see me at the Park Central," he says. "Finish your cheesecake, Charlie."

Arnold Rothstein was shot at the Park Central Hotel that night. The war for what he left behind is where Moonshine Kingdom begins.
